Could AI Finally Save Us From Hold Music?

Two of the fastest-moving AI agents both shipped phone-calling features within hours of each other last week. Instinct launched a service called Concierge, and Meta's Muse rolled out outbound calls to US businesses. Both work roughly the same way— you tell your AI agent what you need, and it dials the business, handles the conversation, and reports back with a transcript and summary.

Google is moving in the same direction with Gemini-powered calling in Search. You ask Google to call a local business, and it'll get you the pricing and availability without you ever picking up the phone yourself.

These agents solves one of the biggest wastes of time most people deal with regularly: waiting on hold.

Here's the wrinkle worth knowing about, though. This week, 404 Media reported that Meta had been quietly routing many of the "AI" Muse calls to human contractors in a call center.

  • A Meta VP acknowledged this was "a miss" and said the company has rolled that back for now, but the reason it was happening in the first place is worth understanding: internal tests reportedly showed human callers hitting a 95-98% success rate on these calls, while the pure-AI version landed noticeably lower.

Which is a pretty good reminder that the tech isn't quite as ready as the marketing suggests. Yet…

My POV: There's a slight worry in the back of my mind about what this kind of technology means for spam calls and scams. Making automated conversations more convincing is going to get messy fast on the receiving end. Imagine being a small business owner fielding AI-placed calls all day, unable to tell which are real customers and which are attempts to social-engineer information out of you.

But honestly? I'm pretty excited to never have to waste three hours on a call with my insurance company where half is spent on hold, and the other half is spent explaining the same problem to three different reps.

Hit reply and let me know: what's the first call you'd hand off to your AI agent?

Google Opens Googlebook Preorders

Via WIRED

Google opened preorders for Googlebook, a new premium laptop category built around Gemini and tighter Android integration.

What’s launching: Googlebook starts at $899, with initial models from Acer, Asus, Dell, HP, and Lenovo. The laptops will run on Intel or Qualcomm processors, offer up to 14 hours of battery life, and pair ChromeOS desktop foundations with the Android technology stack.

Gemini at the center: Google is positioning Googlebook as a laptop built for an AI-first workflow, not just a Chromebook with a nicer spec sheet. Gemini tools will help users draft and organize text, ask questions about what’s on their screen, and resume tasks from an Android phone.

  • Google wants the laptop to feel like part of the same AI-powered ecosystem as your phone, not a separate device you occasionally sync. The idea is to make moving between an Android phone and a laptop feel much more seamless.

Competitive angle: The pricing is interesting. Google is pushing upmarket with an $899 laptop line just as Apple has moved downmarket with the $699 MacBook Neo. That puts both companies closer to the middle: Apple trying to win more budget-conscious buyers, and Google trying to convince premium Android users they deserve a laptop as polished as a MacBook-iPhone setup.

“The bigger picture: The laptop is becoming another front in the AI ecosystem race. Google, Apple, Microsoft, and Meta are all trying to put hardware, software, assistants, and personal context under one roof. The question is whether an all-in-one AI experience will be compelling enough to make people switch devices.“ —

Meta’s Muse Surges Past ChatGPT

Meta’s Muse AI personal agent climbed to the top of the US iOS free app store less than two weeks after launch, briefly overtaking ChatGPT and beating rival AI apps like Claude and Grok.

Fast start: Muse reached 730,000 downloads in roughly the first five days after its Sept.8 debut and more than 2.5 million downloads by Monday, Sensor Tower said.

What Muse does: Meta is pitching Muse as a personal AI agent that can manage tasks across the web, including filling out forms, organizing inboxes, and taking action on users’ behalf. The app is powered by Meta’s Muse Spark family of models and is available for free, with paid subscription tiers at $20 or $100 per month depending on usage.

Agent friction: Muse is also running into the messy reality of agents acting on the internet. Amazon blocked Muse from accessing its shopping site, citing privacy and security risks, while Shopify partnered with Meta to allow agentic checkout in online stores.

“The bigger picture: Muse fits directly into Meta’s broader superintelligence push. The company is not just trying to build smarter models; it wants AI agents that normal people actually use. If Muse keeps gaining traction, Meta could turn its consumer scale into a serious advantage in the agent race.“ —
