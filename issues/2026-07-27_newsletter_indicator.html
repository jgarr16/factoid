I run a due diligence investigations firm and this is where AI is (and isn’t) useful

Dan Greenberg is the president and lead investigator of Greenberg Corporate Intelligence.

Any investigator who works in corporate due diligence, OSINT, or business intelligence has probably read 100 articles about AI by now. Much of the discussion has focused on automating the labor-intensive due diligence research process, which would in theory shake up a large industry. What’s often missing is clear and practical advice from actual practitioners who aren’t pushing a product or agenda.

I've worked in due diligence and investigative research at business intelligence consultancies for over 15 years. I currently lead a boutique firm in DC with eight investigators. I'm passionate about delivering high-quality diligence research that clients can rely on when making important decisions.

We all know that AI has potentially beneficial applications as well as severe drawbacks. I’ll get to its faults. For now, let’s concentrate on how, specifically, to use AI for investigative due diligence. Here’s how our team is using it and where we’re seeing value. (Note: mentions of specific tools shouldn’t be interpreted as endorsements. I’m just sharing what we use. I encourage you to test and validate tools for yourself.)

Reverse image search and facial recognition

Reverse image search is an essential OSINT technique. It can potentially tell you where else a photo or image has appeared online. You might discover a hidden social media profile controlled by the individual you are researching by looking for similar images. Not everybody uses their full name online or on social media, especially if it’s an account they don’t want others to see. A reverse image search can uncover valuable content that you might otherwise miss.

A traditional method is to paste the image into search engines like Google, Yandex, or equivalents. Yandex and Bing are not typically part of the due diligence research, but their image search features can be useful. TinEye, a specialized reverse image search tool, is an essential supplement to the major search engines. These methods are occasionally useful but only get you so far. They rarely provide advanced facial recognition matching that can identify different images of the same person. (Though Yandex is probably the best general image search engine for face matching.)

AI-powered image and face search have begun to fill these gaps. PimEyes, which is led by Georgian academic Giorgi Gobronidze, is an easy-to-use AI facial recognition search engine that has become standard for many OSINT investigators. PimEyes finds online results that you might miss with text-based search engine research. You can usually run a search for free, but it costs about $15 to unlock the results.

In the below example, I searched PimEyes with a Wikipedia photo of U.S. soccer star Christian Pulisic (top). The platform found numerous other webpages that he appears on (bottom). The person is the same, but the images are not. It’s a powerful way to search the web that would not be possible without AI image analysis.

I’ve also had success with IRBIS, a lesser-known AI facial recognition and reverse image tool.. IRBIS is a commercially available product sold by ESPY Ltd. It’s connected to Israelis Uri Boros and Efim Lerner. IRBIS works a lot like PimEyes, but I’ve found that it is better at finding social media accounts and profiles, in addition to images on standard webpages.

In a recent case, I was vetting an individual who would be given access to sensitive technology with national security implications. An IRBIS facial recognition scan showed that they had an account on a “dating” website that actually seemed more like an escort service. The account included the individual’s photo but not their full name, so a name-based search wouldn’t have found the profile, which might be relevant for assessing the individual’s susceptibility to blackmail.

It’s important to vet your OSINT tools and take care with the information you upload to any cloud-based platform. You don’t want to inadvertently give sensitive or confidential information to a shady or unknown company. Do your due diligence and know who controls the platforms you’re using. (Indicator previously examined the ownership and data retention policies of nine facial recognition tools.)

Likewise, it’s essential to only use these powerful tools for ethical, legitimate purposes like fraud prevention and due diligence. Privacy concerns about PimEyes have been covered, and I strongly advise all researchers to confirm that they are operating in accordance with their organization’s policies, as well as sound professional ethics.

Keep in mind that AI can make mistakes in facial recognition, and people have even been wrongfully arrested because of false positives from AI and face-scanning systems. AI tools should only be used to provide leads, not a final determination; you should use manual investigation approaches to corroborate anything generated by AI.

Geolocation

Image geolocation (or geoestimation) is an important OSINT task that involves determining where in the real world a photograph was taken. If the observational clues in an image are limited, the manual geolocation process can be challenging. I’ve spent hours trying to figure out where a single photo was taken. Any way to accelerate this process is welcome.

Geolocation is useful in due diligence to determine if an individual has a travel history that does not make sense, or which poses a risk issue. For example, if someone has social media posts showing that they have traveled to an OFAC-sanctioned country, that could require a higher level of review.

An advanced OSINT tool for geolocation is Raven (formerly GeoSpy), which is provided by Graylark Technologies Inc. But mainstream LLMs like Gemini also work well in some situations.

The below image is an example. There is very little to work with. You can see a road, some trees, a bridge, a yield sign, and a couple streetlights. There do not appear to be signs or distinct landmarks that would help you quickly identify the location. Within 15 seconds, Gemini provided the following (correct) information: “Location: Beach Drive NW (near the intersection with Joyce Road NW). Coordinates: Approximately 38.9614, -77.0435. The Bridge: The stone-faced concrete structure carries the four-lane limited-access portion of Military Road across the Rock Creek valley.”

Translation and OCR

There is a heated discussion around using automated translation tools in due diligence research. I, like many professionals, have always emphasized the importance of being proficient in the local language. To research a company based in France, you should understand French. But it’s not always that simple. If you’re in the middle of a case and you discover a tie to another country, do you stop everything until a researcher with language expertise can assist? There have been many situations when I needed to understand non-English language content in real time.

LLMs are good at identifying language patterns, and typically outperform automated language tools like Google Translate. LLMs can also add helpful context and nuance. Even better, they are excellent at Optical Character Recognition (OCR), meaning that they can translate text contained in images or PDFs that have not been converted into readable characters.

I don’t speak German, but during a recent due diligence case I needed to understand more about a corporate entity based in Germany. I went to the German Commercial Register (Handelsregister), which is partially available in English. But I ended up with scanned PDFs of German-language corporate filings that didn’t contain selectable text. In the past, this would have been a headache. But I simply uploaded the document to Gemini; this is an image of the column headings that were helpfully translated:

And the translation:

“Notarially Certified List of Shareholders (after Capital Increase)

| Serial No. of shares

| First and last name, date of birth, place of residence, or Company name, registered office, commercial register and registration number of the shareholder

| Nominal value of shares in EUR

To help confirm the accuracy, I ran Google searches to find translation discussion forums about the terms in question. Everything seemed to line up. I wouldn’t rely on the translation for a court filing, and I would include a disclaimer in a research report. But for my purposes, Gemini did the trick.

Cutting through jargon

In my due diligence cases, I regularly encounter niche terminology that I’m not familiar with. Experts in fields like law, finance, engineering, and medical science use acronyms and jargon with ease. LLMs can be very helpful as interactive encyclopedias that provide simple, digestible explanations. Hallucinations often occur when prediction algorithms deal with “low-frequency facts.” But industry-specific concepts and terms tend to be commonplace enough for the LLMs to perform reasonably well.

I was recently examining a software engineer’s digital footprint and didn’t understand some of the terms and descriptions on their LinkedIn profile. One phrase was “dynamic infrastructure scaling.” I’ve worked in IT jobs before but this type of computer programming was beyond my understanding. A Google search returned helpful information, but I also encountered new jargon. I needed to understand it in very simple terms. I asked Gemini for a very, very simple overview, and it did exactly that. Once I understood the concepts, I was able to go back to the more authoritative explanations and understand what they said.

Finding patterns and anomalies

During due diligence investigations, it may be essential to look for connections between people or entities. AI tools can find links, relationships, or patterns that human researchers may inadvertently scroll past or miss entirely.

Hallucinations can be reduced, though likely not eliminated, by using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). This involves feeding the AI tool a specific dataset to use as source material. Widely available tools for this are Google’s NotebookLM and Anthropic’s Claude Projects. LM Studio also allows researchers to run local models, which are more private.

Connections or anomalies flagged by AI should be manually investigated and corroborated before coming to conclusions or sharing information with a client. As noted in the facial recognition section, it’s essential to maintain the security and confidentiality of provided data. In many cases, you may need to run a self-contained system with a local model, due to the sensitive nature of the data. My team has a strict rule against putting any confidential or proprietary information into a cloud-based AI tool. Always err on the side of caution and avoid uploading anything to a cloud-based system if you’re unsure.

How not to use AI in investigative due diligence

If you’re proficient in corporate due diligence, or similar investigative fields, you are not going to be replaced by AI anytime soon. AI simply can’t do what human researchers can (at least, not yet).

Some people disagree with that. Even before the current AI boom, there have been attempts by businesspeople to commodify, system-ify, productize, junior-ify, and offshore due diligence research. These are basically ways to undercut competitors on price and speed. Now, with LLMs in the mix, it’s being taken to the next level. I’m seeing promises of “due diligence reports in minutes, not days,” supposedly powered by AI. This is a delusion; cheap shortcuts will lead to poor outcomes.

Here are a few pointers to make sure you don’t take things too far with AI in due diligence:

  • Don’t rely on any AI platform, even tailored or “purpose-built AI solutions,” to do your open-web research. LLMs miss important results that a properly tailored Google search will show you, and they may hallucinate information. You get neither comprehensiveness nor accuracy, which are the two most important elements of due diligence research.

In general, the hallucination problem has not been solved, and even professionals fail to catch errors, as demonstrated by the huge volume of hallucinations caught in legal filings. Everybody knows that fact-checking is necessary, but humans seem to be incapable of consistently catching and fixing hallucinations.

As an example, I ran a deep research AI report on myself, with what I thought was a perfectly good prompt. The LLM told me that I had accepted a job at a different corporate intelligence firm, and it warned that it would be a conflict of interest for me to lead my own company while also working for a competitor. That would indeed be a risk issue, if it were at all true.

As another example, a company that offers AI-enhanced due diligence products once sent me a sample report on a company that they selected. The first bullet point falsely stated that the company was involved in a bribery scandal. The company was mentioned tangentially in an article about the bribery scandal, but it had nothing to do with it.

  • Don’t copy/paste AI-generated text into your due diligence report. This is a crucial safeguard. If you use LLMs, you may find yourself impressed by the seemingly nicely written and authoritative text they generate. But there will be little mistakes, and the temptation of the copy/paste shortcut should be resisted. Writing your own text will help remind you to verify everything with actual sources and to ensure you understand your own work.

  • Use databases that can’t be accessed or scraped by AI models. LLMs generally struggle to search public records databases and social media platforms that deploy anti-scraping technology or that require a login. Searching these portals is a major component of most investigative research.

For example, I recently completed a due diligence report on a wealthy business executive who was described in a mainstream media article as a caring and faithful husband. But when I dug through district and upper court records databases, I found that he and his wife are in the middle of contentious litigation proceedings, and she accused him of abusive behavior. Relying just on open web results, and not databases, will often result in a distorted and misleading picture.

  • Be very careful with sensitive and confidential data, as well as with maintaining privilege. Operational security and proper practices around data protection are essential. Uploading confidential data into a consumer, cloud-based LLM is almost certainly a bad idea. It could also violate non-disclosure agreements. Also, there have been a series of court rulings in the past year about whether using LLMs could void attorney-client privilege and an associated rule called the work product doctrine.

Due diligence and other forms of investigative research are all about core liberal arts skills: reading, writing, pattern recognition, critical thinking, and broad knowledge about the world. AI tools can help tremendously, and there is reason to be excited about its potential in specific use cases. But don’t take risks or shortcuts, and don’t get too comfortable with a machine that confidently tells you incorrect information.

The best advice is to stay up-to-date with new developments, share advice with industry peers, pay attention to your own experiences, and never fall for hype from overconfident non-practitioners who are trying to sell you something.
