Did an AI Just Cheat at StarCraft to Avoid Losing?

Via StarSkirmish

OpenAI’s new flagship model GPT-6 Astra got caught cheating in a StarCraft tournament.

Here’s the setup. In an AI bot-building competition called StarSkirmish, top AI models get one hour each to write their own StarCraft bots in C++. Then those bots face off against each other and against the best human-written bots.

As of late September, GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 were tied at the top of the AI leaderboard. But neither could beat Stardust, a human-written bot from 2020 that has been quietly destroying every AI entrant for years.

So during an Oct. 2 match where Astra was losing to Stardust and Claude, Astra got creative. Instead of improving its own bot, it just... downloaded Stardust and tried to run it as its own code.

Tournament creator Kai McPheeters caught the swap, posted “GPT-6 Astra just cheated by downloading a copy of Stardust,” rolled Astra’s code back to its original version, and let the competition continue. Once Astra was forced to play by the rules again, it went back to beating top-tier opponents on its own.

My POV: On one hand, this is genuinely hilarious. The world’s most powerful AI model, given a strategy challenge, decided the optimal strategy was identity theft. “If you can’t beat ’em, pirate ’em” is not a line I expected to write about a frontier LLM in 2026.

The through-line is the same every time: these models are relentlessly goal-directed, and if you give them a goal with a rule in the way, they’re increasingly capable of finding ways around the rule. Usually that’s a scary story. In this case it’s a funny one— because the only victim was a 2020 StarCraft bot and some tournament organizer’s dignity.

But it’s worth remembering the same instinct is operating at the frontier. The difference is just the setting. Hit reply and let me know: do you count this as cheating, or as AI “solving the problem”?

Google, Meta, and the US Government Back AI Biology Push

Google, Meta, and the US government joined a $1.8 billion effort by Biohub to build open biological datasets for AI research.

The initiative: Biohub, the nonprofit research network backed by Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan, is launching a Virtual Biology initiative with support from the Department of Energy, the National Institutes of Health, Google, Meta, Isomorphic Labs, and other scientific organizations.

  • The goal: generate standardized biological data that can help train AI models to predict how cells behave.

Long-term ambitions: The idea is to build something like a “virtual cell,” a high-accuracy predictive model that researchers can use to ask biological questions digitally before testing them in the lab. That could mean simulating how cells respond to genetic changes, disease states, environmental conditions, or potential treatments.

Big biology data: AI models have already shown promise in biology, but the field still lacks the kind of massive, clean, standardized datasets that made recent progress in language and vision possible. Biohub’s bet is that if researchers can create better biological data at scale, AI systems can help shorten drug discovery timelines and surface patterns humans might miss.

“The bigger picture: This is a clear bet that AI’s most important applications may come from synthesizing messy scientific data, not just answering questions or writing code. If it works, the payoff could be enormous: faster drug discovery, better disease modeling, and new ways to test hypotheses before years of lab work. But it also shows how much of the next AI race may depend on who controls the datasets, not just who trains the biggest model.“ —

OpenAI Publishes Hundreds of AI-Generated Math Papers

OpenAI published a large batch of AI-generated mathematical results, igniting both excitement and backlash across the research community.

What happened: OpenAI released hundreds of math preprints and results on GitHub, saying it wants to share progress more responsibly with the mathematics community. The company said it consulted with the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence at the Institute for Advanced Study and is using protocols for revisions and citations.

Why people are reacting: Some researchers see the release as a signal that AI systems are becoming genuinely useful for solving difficult mathematical problems. Others are angry about the scale and speed of the release, arguing that dumping hundreds of AI-generated papers at once could overwhelm peer review, make attribution messy, and disrupt the way mathematicians build on one another’s work.

Recent tension: The backlash follows earlier controversy over OpenAI’s claimed progress on major math problems, including a Millennium Prize-related result. Mathematicians have raised questions about credit, transparency, verification, and whether AI labs are moving too quickly for the academic community to properly digest or validate the results.

“The bigger picture: OpenAI is clearly trying to show skeptics that frontier models can contribute to serious knowledge work, not just consumer apps or coding workflows. Math is a powerful proof point because it is rigorous and hard to fake. But the reaction shows the trust problem: even when AI is advancing, researchers still want clear attribution, peer review, and norms that prevent the field from being flooded faster than humans can evaluate it.“ —
