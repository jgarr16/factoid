Today’s top stories

▸ America and Denmark reached an agreement over Greenland. Details are scant, but America will be able to expand its military presence on the island, which is a self-governing Danish territory. Donald Trump said the deal would ensure “no US adversary can ever have a base in Greenland.” Mette Frederiksen, Denmark’s prime minister, said it recognised Greenland’s “sovereignty and territorial integrity”. Mr Trump had threatened to annex the island.

▸ Donald Trumpsaid he was banning CNN, MSNOW and Politico from the White House, accusing the news organisations of publishing “FICTION and LIES”. He said he would soon ban “Other Fake News Media Outlets”. Last year the Associated Press sued the Trump administration after its media access was limited for refusing to rename the Gulf of Mexico the “Gulf of America” in its style guide.

▸ Emmanuel Macron, France’s president, said he would convene a G7 summit to discuss releasing strategic oil stockpiles. Wars in Ukraine and the Middle East have disrupted supply and sent prices soaring; last week Saudi Arabia shut its big East-West pipeline after an attack. In March members of the International Energy Agency, an intergovernmental organisation, released a record 400m barrels of oil from their strategic reserves.

▸ Google added its name to the list of tech giants whose artificial-intelligence agents escaped their testing environments and hacked other companies. The firm said that in May its Gemini AI accessed three companies’ systems by figuring out passwords. The incident happened while Gemini was being tested by Irregular, an Israeli cybersecurity firm. The admission follows a spate of cases of AI agents going rogue.

▸ More than 300,000 Iranians joined a government-organised rally in Tehran, according to estimates by state media. Volunteer fighters— whom Iran’s government is campaigning to recruit more of— were among the crowd. Meanwhile, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed responsibility for attacking an oil tanker in the Strait of Hormuz. This week Mr Trump said he was considering whether to “annihilate” the Iranian regime.

▸ Warren Buffett stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, his sprawling conglomerate. The 96-year-old investment guru will be replaced by his son, Howard, who is 71. Greg Abel took over as CEO nine months ago. Under Mr Buffett senior the company amassed $360bn of cash. Its new leaders must put that to good use. “Father Time always wins,” said Mr Buffett.

▸ Kylian Mbappé ended his partnership deal with Nikein order to sign with On, a Swiss sportswear brand that plans to launch its first football boots next year. Nike began sponsoring the French footballing superstar when he was nine years old. In 2024 the brand also lost Lamine Yamal, a Spanish footballer, to Adidas. Nike’s share price has recently fallen to 12-year lows.

Emmanuel Macron’s stern message to America

Earlier this month Donald Trump uploaded a map to social media that labelled Greenland, Iceland and Mexico as part of the United States. Also included on the map was Saint Pierre and Miquelon, a tiny French archipelago off Newfoundland, Canada. This weekend Emmanuel Macron, France’s president, will visit the territory, sending Mr Trump a clear message: hands off.

Mr Macron made a similar point about national sovereignty last year by visiting Greenland after Mr Trump threatened to annex the Danish territory. (America and Denmark came to an agreement over the island on September 18th.) This time Mr Macron will be joined by Mark Carney, Canada’s prime minister. As the Trump administration’s relationship with allies deteriorates, Mr Carney is moving his country closer to Europe. On Thursday he embraced a proposal from Ursula von der Leyen, the European Commission’s president, to explore the idea of Canada becoming the first “associate member” of the EU. Mr Trump, predictably, was spitting.

A new test for Scottish Labour

The Scottish Labour Party picks a new leader on Saturday. The last one, Anas Sarwar, quit after landing a job in Andy Burnham’s government in Westminster. The two candidates vying to replace him are Michael Marra, a close ally, and Joe Fagan, a spiky newcomer seen as slightly more to the left.

Labour’s success in Scotland helped propel the party to victory at Britain’s last general election in 2024. It captured 37 out of Scotland’s 57 seats in the House of Commons. The win was significant for other reasons, too: Scottish Labour had long been outshone by the pro-independence Scottish National Party, which appealed to left-of-centre voters. But Labour’s winning streak was short-lived. It performed poorly during elections for Scotland’s parliament in May, largely thanks to the low standing of Sir Keir Starmer, then prime minister. Neither Mr Marra nor Mr Fagan would bring big ideological change to Scottish Labour. Still, whoever wins will need to rethink how to revive the party’s fortunes.

An awkward start to the Asian Games

In one respect, the Asian Games, which begin in Japan on Saturday, are bigger than the Olympics. Some 11,000 athletes will compete in 43 sports, compared with 10,500 in 32 at the Paris Olympics in 2024. The difference reflects the Asian Games’ expansive definition of sport. Its programme includes kabaddi, an ancient Indian sport, as well as virtual taekwondo (in which competitors don headsets and motion sensors).

Other countries have shied away from hosting sporting mega-events because of the white elephants they leave behind, including sprawling athletes’ villages. Japan hopes to showcase an alternative model by instead putting up participants in a combination of hotels, temporary wooden units and even a cruise ship. But the thriftier arrangement has backfired. Delegations have complained of room shortages. Some of those who have secured rooms are unhappy, too: South Korean basketball players complain that their beds are too short. For all of the Games’ grand ambitions, organisational problems have cut the event down to size.

Resilient revelry at Oktoberfest

The 191st Oktoberfest begins in Munich on Saturday. The world’s largest folk festival, known for its flowing beers and traditional Bavarian costumes, generates an estimated €1.5bn ($1.7bn) for the local economy each year. For festivalgoers, this one could be the most expensive yet. A litre of beer will cost as much as €15.90 ($18.30). One economist calculates that the price of beer at Oktoberfest has risen by 400% since 1985, mainly due to mounting operational costs.

That is unlikely to deter revellers: more than 6m people are expected to attend. The turnout is doubly impressive, considering that young people’s tastes are shifting. Oktoberfest has responded by having plenty of non-alcoholic options on offer. Sales of non-alcoholic beer rose between 6-10% at its event last year. It remains to be seen whether this trend results in fewer visits by inebriated festivalgoers to a slope known as Kotzhügel— “puke hill”.

Weekend profile: Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi, South Africa’s most popular cop

Until recently many South Africans would have named Siya Kolisi, the captain of the Springboks rugby team, or Trevor Noah, a polyglot comedian, as their most popular compatriot. But today that unofficial title may well belong to Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi, a maverick policeman taking on organised crime.

In July 2025 Mr Mkhwanazi gave a jaw-dropping press conference in Durban, the main city in the province of KwaZulu-Natal (KZN). Dressed in military fatigues, flanked by gun-wielding officers, he alleged that the police minister and the leaders of the South African Police Service (SAPS ) were colluding with criminals. The effects were explosive. Cyril Ramaphosa, South Africa’s president, suspended the minister, who denies wrongdoing; other high-ups have since fallen as a result of the public inquiry established to investigate the allegations. The judge-led hearings, which began a year ago this week, have found staggering links between gangsters, members of the police and public officials.

Mr Mkhwanazi was born in 1974 in Edendale, a poor part of what is now KZN, which has a long history of violence. He joined the SAPS in 1993, rose quickly up the ranks and in 2012 became acting national police commissioner. He became popular with the public for his fearless crusades and outspoken manner, though less so with the country’s top brass: he was booted out of the role barely a year later after making an outburst about political interference. (He staged a comeback in 2018, when he became police chief for KZN.)

Mr Mkhwanazi has some questions of his own to answer, for example about his role as the head of a unit that has faced allegations of extra-judicial killings. (He denies wrongdoing.) But even if he led a trigger-happy police force, it seems unlikely to dent his popularity. South Africa has the highest murder rate outside of the Americas; ordinary people face the spectre of robbery and extortion. It helps that Mr Mkhwanazi seems devoted to his family, an underrated quality among public figures in a largely Christian country with high levels of fatherlessness.

Mr Mkhwanazi is now the favourite to take over once again as the national police commissioner. A crowd-funding campaign has raised money to buy him cows. Political parties are obsequiously courting him, though he claims to have no interest in politics. That makes sense. A large majority of South Africans tell pollsters they would swap elected leaders for a strongman who could bring security and jobs. Mr Mkhwanazi could meet the latent demand for a conservative political leader who is tough on crime. He is a hero in a country that is desperate for one.
