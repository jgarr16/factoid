Today’s top stories

▸ America’sSupreme Court rejected Donald Trump’s attempt to restrictmail-in voting ahead of November’s midterm elections. Mr Trump claims, without evidence, that postal voting is prone to fraud. In March he ordered the postal service to curtail ballot deliveries. A federal judge blocked the directive, saying it could disenfranchise voters. In an unsigned ruling, the Supreme Court’s justices kept that order in place.

▸ The yield on American ten-year Treasuries exceeded 5%, its highest level since 2023. Rising oil prices, high levels of government debt and competition for capital from the private sector have all pushed up borrowing costs. Although the Treasury is buying back more bonds, that has done little to contain yields. The sell-off threatens the bull market in equities, too.

▸ Donald Trumprejected calls for greater regulation of artificial intelligence, insisting America’s government had “tremendous” control over the leading AI labs. He criticised Dario Amodei after the boss of Anthropic warned the development of the most advanced models should be slowed. Meanwhile Microsoftpublished a draft code of conduct for training its models, which says AI should not imitate consciousness, nor assist with the development of weapons.

▸ China tightened rules on overseas travelfor its citizens to “safeguard national sovereignty and security”. The regulations allow the government to ban people from leaving the country if they are deemed to have “harmed” China’s interests, for example by violating tech export regulations. The government insisted that the “preventative” measures would not “restrict the travel of ordinary citizens”.

▸ America’s Environmental Protection Agencyproposed scrapping rules that limit greenhouse-gas emissions from coal- and gas-fired power plants. The EPA says that repealing Biden-administration requirements that plants reduce pollution by using technologies like carbon capture and storage would save $310bn. It also wants to block future administrations from regulating the emissions.

▸ Mr Trump saidRussia and Ukrainehad agreed not to attack each other’s energy facilities. Volodymyr Zelensky, Ukraine’s president, said he had proposed the truce but was “not confident” Russia would honour it. Earlier Mark Rutte, NATO’s secretary-general, pledged additional support for Ukraine after a Russian drone attack close to the border with Poland.

▸ “Widow’s Bay” was the big winner at the Primetime Emmy’s Awards, a television-industry shindig in Los Angeles. Apple TV’s comedy-horror series won 14 awards, more than any other show. It was a big night for Apple, which won the most Emmy’s for the first time. Rhea Seehorn, the lead in “Pluribus”, another one of its hit series, took home the award for best actress.

The day ahead

GETTY IMAGES

Amodei and Altman take the stage

The most powerful AI bosses will speak at a conference run by Salesforce, a software company, in San Francisco that begins on Tuesday. It will be one of the first public appearances by Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s chief executive, since he called for tech firms to slow down their development of powerful systems lest they pose a risk to humanity.

Mr Amodei has urged labs to focus on understanding their systems and making them more consistently ethical. Worries around safety threaten to delay his company’s plans to go public, in what may be the biggest IPO of all time.

Sam Altman, OpenAI ’s boss and another speaker at the event, agrees. He says that the safety debate means his company is unlikely to list before next year— although few people expected it to be sooner. When the rivals last appeared together, at a summit earlier this year in India, they declined to link hands for a group photo. This is another unusual moment of alignment.

AP

Scott Bessent’s latest test

Today America’s Senate will vote on whether to advance the Clarity Act, sweeping legislation aimed at creating a regulatory framework for cryptocurrency markets. There has been a particularly bitter fight over how to regulate stablecoins, cryptocurrencies designed to maintain a stable value, which banks fret could eat into their deposits, and thus profits. Scott Bessent, the treasury secretary, has warned that a failure to support the act would show “America is unwilling to lead” on digital assets.

Mr Bessent has a last chance to make his case on Tuesday morning, when he appears before the House Financial Services committee for a yearly grilling. Members have plenty of other topics to ask about, however, not least the treasury department’s misfiring attemptsto contain borrowing costs, and its more successful interventions to support the yen. The Trump administration has had mixed results winning over markets recently. Mr Bessent will hope that lawmakers are more amenable.

Brazil’s tarnished defenders of democracy

Brazil’s Supreme Court is in crisis. At its heart is Alexandre de Moraes, the judge who oversaw the prosecution of Jair Bolsonaro, a former populist-right president who is serving a 27-year sentence for attempting a coup after losing his bid for re-election in 2022.

Mr Moraes is under scrutiny for his links to Daniel Vorcaro, the man behind the biggest bank fraud in Brazilian history. Another judge, who was appointed by Mr Bolsonaro, recently leaked messages between the two. Mr Moraes’s wife, a lawyer, had an unusually vague and expensive contract to represent Mr Vorcaro’s bank. His wife’s law firm has denied any impropriety.

On Tuesday the court will decide whether to open a criminal investigation into Mr Moraes, who denies wrongdoing. But any ruling may be undone after October 4th, when Brazil holds a presidential election. Mr Bolsonaro’s son, Flávio, is the right’s leading candidate who is polling just behind the left-wing president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. He has promised to pardon his father and purge the highest court if he wins.

REUTERS

Lessons of the Lucy Letby murder case

On Tuesday an independent public inquiry in Britain will publish its final report on the institutional failures at a hospital in north-west England where Lucy Letby, a former nurse, was found guilty of murdering seven babies and attempting to murder seven others in 2015-16. The report will suggest changes to Britain’s health-care system to stop a similar tragedy from occurring.

Yet doubts persist over Ms Letby’s conviction. Medical experts have disputed the evidence. Statisticians have flagged the dangers of confusing the low probability of a sequence of events with a high likelihood of guilt. None of the experts claims that Ms Letby is innocent. But if her conviction was based on unreliable evidence, it could be unsafe in the eyes of the law. Her legal team argue she is the victim of the “biggest miscarriage of justice” in British history. The case continues to raise unsettling questions about both the British health-care and justice systems.
