Block’s human-sounding voice AI runs on laptops: Jack Dorsey’s fintech company just unveiled Dessa, a full-duplex model trained entirely on human speech. Unlike most voice systems, it doesn’t need an external LLM and works directly with audio, picking up sarcasm, laughter, and interruptions along the way. Block says it can run locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max or better. Access is limited to safety researchers for now, but you can hear how human it sounds in the demos.

Moonshot’s new open model closes in on frontier coding: The Chinese AI startup just dropped Kimi K3.1 Pro, a 1M-token context model that scored 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, per the company. That puts it ahead of GPT-6 Astra and just behind Claude Opus 5.5, at $2/$10 per million input/output tokens. It handles long-horizon coding tasks, from quick bug fixes to building games in Unity and Godot. Moonshot also deliberately limited its offensive cyber skills by removing hacking-related data from the training set. Try it here, with open weights arriving by Nov. 4.

Tailscale SSH flaws could hand hackers root access across servers: If your team relies on the mesh VPN for SSH, you will want to pay attention to this. Researcher Erica Windisch just published 11 flaws in its software and the self-hosted headscale server. In her lab, one hacked device abused SSH port forwarding to take full control of other machines and add a rogue device to the network. Tailscale says fixes are coming, but there’s no patch yet. For now, audit SSH access and disable unnecessary forwarding.

How a GitHub staff engineer estimates work he says can’t be estimated

Source: The Code, Superhuman

You know the drill. Someone asks for a ship date before you even know what is lurking in the code. In complex systems, research eats up most of your time, making accurate estimates impossible. GitHub staff engineer Sean Goedecke agrees with this and in an essay he shows how he still finds a way to give an estimate anyway.

He starts with the date. To Goedecke, estimates are tools for deciding which projects get funded or cut. Managers often have a timeline in mind before the design exists, so he works backwards from it.

His process looks like this:

  • Read the room first. Before touching the code, gauge how much pressure is behind the request. A CTO’s one-week deadline and a casual ask for call need very different answers.

  • Start with the target. Ask what version of the project could fit the timeline already on the table.

  • Price in the unknowns. Every "dark forest" in your codebase brings extra risk. Build in extra buffer, or reshape your plan around the parts of the code your team knows well.

  • Give people choices. Give them a few options, each with its own level of risk. You can take on the tough parts and risk losing a month, find a workaround, or bring in a team that already knows the code.

The real skill is earning trust. Your manager picks the tradeoff. Goedecke saves “this is impossible” for the rare cases where it really is, because people stop listening to someone who pushes back on every estimate.

There is one big catch. Companies often treat estimates as if they transfer between teams. In Goedecke’s experience, some teams ship the same work ten times faster than others, so a number means little until it’s clear who is doing the work. If you’re ready to go deeper, check out Goedecke's piece, "Engineers Who Won't Commit," to learn how to make the call under uncertainty.
