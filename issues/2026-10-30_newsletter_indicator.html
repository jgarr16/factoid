Tools & Tips

Image supplied by Discord

Discord is becoming (a bit) more discoverable.

The company announced that content in Discoverable Servers will soon appear on public Server Web Pages and in web search results.

Discoverable Servers are public servers that anyone can join. They have 1,000 or more members, are at least eight weeks old, and meet Discord’s activity and safety requirements. A Server Web Page is a publicly accessible website associated with a Discoverable Server. Pages were previously optional and only displayed basic information about a server.

Starting November 16, every Discoverable Server will get a Server Web Page, and those pages may show automated summaries of discussions in public channels. In mid-December, messages from public servers will begin to appear in search results. (Admins can choose which channels take part or opt all of them out, and individual users can stop their own messages from appearing on the web.

In an announcement, Discord’s Kelsey Duncombe-Smith said that “valuable information [is] being locked in Discord conversations, inaccessible to the wider internet. We want to fix this.”

She added:

“From mid-December 2026, answers to questions your members sent in public channels can be found in a web search, in their own words. Server admins decide which channels take part, and server members can choose whether their own messages appear. Usernames and avatars will not be shown alongside these messages.“ — Discord

This is good news for investigators because Discord server content has been largely absent from web search results.. The changes mean that some — emphasis on some — server content will begin showing up in web search results. (Private servers and DMs remain private and unindexed.)

One notable limitation is that member messages are excluded from the web by default in the EU, UK, Canada, Brazil, Australia, and South Korea due to “legal restrictions.” Users have to opt in.

Discord currently has more than 63,000 Discoverable Servers. — Craig

📍 Foeke Postma and Miguel Ramalho of Bellingcat ran a new battery of tests to see how well LLMs perform geolocation. (See their previous posts here and here.)

They tested 52 photos with models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Mistral, Google, Moonshot AI, and other companies. Read the full post, but here’s part of their conclusion:

“Our tests show that the coordinates models provide as answers are rarely an exact match, even with supercharged models. Invariably, even the best models, with the best tools, can confidently claim a wrong position …

Though it can speed up geolocation work significantly, especially in narrowing down an area of interest, AI models remain a useful assistant rather than a tool we can blindly delegate geolocations to.“ — Bellingcat

Reports & Research

📍 Marc Owen Jones and Sohan Dsouza published an extensive investigation into a massive online influence campaign pushing Islamophobic ads across multiple platforms. Meta disrupted an earlier iteration, but the campaign continued. The campaign has not been attributed, but several indicators point to Israel-based operators. The report estimated an ad spend budget of several million euros and said the campaign reached more than half a billion views on Facebook, TikTok, and YouTube. (Le Monde has a write-up in French).

📍 On Tech Policy Press, Lila Bailey of the Internet Archive and Tess Vartanian of the Center for Democracy and Technology argued that there may be unintended consequences in “sweeping prohibitions on anonymous scraping,” like the ones currently being proposed in the United States. “The spread of AI crawling has created legitimate challenges for publishers and website operators,” they wrote. But “many of the most socially valuable uses of crawling require anonymity.”

📍 NewsGuard found that “ChatGPT Images 2.5, produces incriminating, realistic images of U.S. midterm candidates based on false claims or accusations about them” in 16 out of 20 attempts. This included photos of Iowa senatorial candidate Ashley Hinson embracing Jeffrey Epstein.

One More Thing

Soon after the release of Christopher Nolan’s Odyssey, Elon Musk declared that Grok AI could make a more accurate version of the epic.

X

So how’d that work out? Musk recently announced its release and Wired gave it a look:

“Grok’s introductory episode bears many hallmarks of AI slop. The movements of faces don’t match the dialog, which is further undercut by stilted and unnatural delivery. Besides Odysseus, none of the principal characters are introduced by name, and the pacing issues are laughable: The Ithacans arrive on the shores of Troy immediately after leaving their homeland, for example. Even the blue checks on X were harsh in their assessment. “Someone actually watched this and still decided to post it?” wrote one user. “Fire that person.”“ — Wired

The magazine also asked Grok if its adaptation was “historically accurate.”

“No,” it replied. “Grok Imagine’s Episode 1 (and the broader promised series) is an adaptation of Homer’s Odyssey, not a historically accurate reconstruction of real events.”
