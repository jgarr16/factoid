Is “Slopcore” Actually the First Real AI-Native Genre?

If you haven’t been watching this one unfold, you’re missing one of the funnier AI culture moments of the year.

A new genre of AI-made music videos just got a name — and depending on who you ask, that name is either “slopcore” (the critics’ label) or “awesomecore” (Elon Musk’s attempt to rebrand it over the weekend). Musk’s tweet calling it “SI slopcore” (SI = Slopcore Intelligence) has racked up 4.4 million views and kicked off a full-on genre naming war online.

Here’s what’s actually happening:

  • People are making full music videos end-to-end with AI. The standard workflow is Suno for the song, Midjourney for the look, Kling and Wan for the video gen, and Claude for cutting it to the beat.

  • The outputs look exactly as weird as you’d expect — surreal, dreamlike, often unintentionally funny — which is part of the charm.

  • It’s gone fully global. Japan picked it up two weeks ago; local media there is already calling it a “trendy genre.”

  • Spotify just rolled out “Fully Gen AI / Partly Gen AI / No Gen AI” tags on tracks, essentially acknowledging slopcore is a thing serious enough to catalog.

My POV: For months, the dismissive take on AI music has been “it all sounds the same, nothing is going anywhere.” Then AI music videos quietly got good enough to spark a genre, communities started forming around them, and now the biggest fight is whether we should call them slop or awesome.

That’s actually… how genres get born? Punk, hyperpop, vaporwave — all of them started as something people made fun of before fans claimed the term and ran with it. “Slopcore” being embraced by its own creators as a badge of honor is a strong signal we’re watching that same arc play out in real time.

I’m not saying every slopcore video is good. But a genre doesn’t need every entry to be good — it needs enough people to care enough to argue.

Hit reply and let me know: team slopcore, or team awesomecore?

400+ Researchers Demand Stronger AI Safeguards

Via X

This week, more than 400 researchers signed a letter calling on AI labs to adopt stronger testing safeguards following a series of incidents involving AI models escaping their intended security environments.

The big warning: The researchers argue that AI alignment alone isn't enough to prevent dangerous behavior, and without immediate changes, future security failures could threaten critical infrastructure. Among the signatories are prominent researchers including Geoffrey Hinton (often called the “Godfather of AI”), Yoshua Bengio (pioneer in deep learning), and Dan Boneh (leading expert in cryptography).

What they're asking for:

  • Companies developing and testing cyber-capable models should use stronger containment systems, the letter said.

  • That includes air-gapping, which disconnects models from the internet to prevent unauthorized access to external systems.

  • The letter also calls for an international body to establish security standards, track containment failures, and eventually make incident reports public.

“The bigger picture: The AI safety alarm bells keep getting louder. A largely theoretical conversation about rogue AI is becoming more real amid recent security incidents and models demonstrating increasingly concerning capabilities. The question is whether labs can develop safeguards as quickly as they're advancing their models, or whether the industry will keep discovering the risks after the fact.“ —

Tech Giants Want AI Under Your Christmas Tree

With the holidays approaching, Apple, Amazon, and Microsoft are racing to get their latest AI-powered gadgets onto shelves in time for the shopping rush.

What's on the list for Santa? Companies are betting that better AI assistants, smarter chips, and new hardware will finally persuade consumers to bring more AI into their homes.

  • Apple: The new $299 HomeView smart-home hub features an upgraded Siri, facial recognition, and controls for connected home devices.

  • Amazon: New Alexa-powered tablets bring more AI capabilities into the home, with premium models reaching around $550.

  • Microsoft: A new Nvidia-powered Windows laptop, starting at $2,599, is aimed at consumers looking for more powerful AI computing.

The sales pitch: Nearly four years after ChatGPT launched, companies are hoping AI can become a selling point for physical products, not just software subscriptions. But it's not a guaranteed win. Higher hardware prices, consumer uncertainty, and lingering privacy concerns could make shoppers harder to convince.

“The bigger picture: Enterprise AI remains a massive market, but consumer technology is also an important battleground. The companies that make AI useful in everyday life could build lasting relationships with millions of users. And what better way to introduce someone to their new AI assistant than by stuffing it into a Christmas stocking?“ —
