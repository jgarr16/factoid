Fable 5.2 vs. Opus 5.5: I tested both on real stocks

The results will surprise you.

Compound With AI

Nov 15

READ IN APP

7 days ago, Anthropic announced its new model, Claude Fable 5.2.

And as always,the internet is full of people telling you how amazing it is.

You’ll see:

  • Posts explaining why it’s a game changer
  • Screenshots of impressive benchmark results.
  • Tutorials showing you how to get the most out of it.

And hear me , there’s nothing wrong with that..

But personally, I don’t pay much attention to benchmarks.

A model that scores higher on a benchmark isn’t necessarily better at what matters to us: understanding businesses, analyzing financials, valuing companies, and finding risks.

So I spent hours testing Fable 5.2 against Opus 5.5 on real companies, using my own Investing Skills.

And the results weren’t what I expected.

Fable5.2 VS Opus 5.5: I tested both on real stocks:

The idea was simple.

  • I took stocks I was already researching and gave both models the same tasks.
  • I ran each test in a separate profile so neither model could benefit from previous research or conversations.
  • Then I read and compared every report, one by one.
  • Yes, it takes time…

But let’s start with the easiest test: simple prompts.

1. One Simple Prompt, 20 Companies: Fable vs. Opus

One of the big promises of smarter AI models is that they should need fewer instructions to deliver good results.

In theory, instead of writing a long, detailed prompt, you should be able to ask a simple question and let the model figure out what matters.

But does this actually work for stock research?

I wanted to test this at the very beginning of my investing process: understanding how a business makes money.

I tested Fable 5.2 against Opus 5.5 across 20 companies from different industries.

Both received exactly the same one-line prompt:

“I want to understand the business model of [company].”

To compare the results, I did evaluate the answers on four criteria: business model understanding, key financial drivers, useful insights, and factual reliability.

I also fact-checked the most important or surprising claims.

The results surprised me:

  • Opus won 12 comparisons, Fable won 7, and one was a tie.
  • Opus averaged 17.5/20, compared with 15.9/20 for Fable.

Both models were good at explaining how businesses make money.

But Opus was more consistent at identifying the numbers that actually drive performance and using the latest company results.

For this simple one-line prompt test, Opus performed better than Fable overall, despite Fable being the more powerful model on paper.

But let’s be clear: no serious investor would rely on a one-line prompt to research a stock.

That was just a test to see how much the models could figure out on their own.

But what happens when you give both models a real investing task?

For serious stock research I use Investing Skills I’ve built and refined over time.

If you’re new here, a Skill is a detailed set of instructions that tells Claude what to research, which sources to use, how to analyze the information, and what format is needed.

I wanted to answer a practical question:

When both models follow the same investing process, where does the smarter model actually make a difference?

To find out, I tested both models using 3 types of Skills:

  1. Business and industry research: Understanding how a company makes money, what drives growth, and how its industry works.
  2. Financial analysis and valuation: Reading 10 years of financial statements, calculating ratios, and testing valuation assumptions.
  3. Challenging investment ideas: Finding weak assumptions, questioning an investment thesis, and spotting risks I may have missed.

These are very different tasks.

Some require gathering and organizing information.

Others require accurate calculations or deeper reasoning.

And that’s exactly why I wanted to test all 3.

1. Business and industry research: Fable goes a little further

I tested 2 of my Investing Skills: Business Overview and Learn Any Industry.

Both are part of the first step of my investing process.

They help me understand how a company makes money, what drives its performance, and how its industry works.

Both models produced very similar research reports :

  • Report length: Both generated reports of similar length, with roughly the same level of detail.
  • Information covered: Both addressed the key questions in the Skills and covered largely the same information.
  • Following instructions: Opus followed the Skill structure more closely. Fable was more likely to go beyond the requested scope.
  • Extra insights: Fable sometimes added relevant information I hadn’t explicitly requested, such as definitions of financial terms or additional takeaways.

For example, in the Nike Business Overview, Fable added a small section explaining terms:

Takeaway:For this type of research, where the main work is gathering, organizing, and explaining information we have a small advantage to Fable.

But no major difference that justifies using Fable with 2X the cost.

2. Financial analysis and valuation: Almost no difference

Afet , I tested both models on financial analysis and valuation using my existing Skills.

This included reading financial statements, analyzing historical trends, calculating ratios, and challenging valuation assumptions.

I couldn’t find a meaningful difference in the quality of the results.

And I think the reason is simple :

My Skills already provide detailed instructions on how to do the analysis, which calculations to make, and how to check the results for errors.

So both models follow the same process, with less room for mistakes.

My takeaway: Opus already does this work very well.

For now, I don’t see a reason to use Fable for these tasks.

3. Challenging an investment thesis: The most interesting test

This is where I expected Fable’s stronger reasoning to make a difference.

I used my Thesis Challenge Skill to ask both models to challenge the same Nike investment thesis, looking for weak assumptions, inconsistencies, and risks.

Both did a good job, but they focused on different issues.

  • Fable focused on valuation consistency. It spotted that the original memo compared a forward exit P/E with historical trailing P/E multiples, making the valuation look more conservative than it really was.
  • Opus focused on business economics. It noticed that Nike’s expected margin recovery depended heavily on revenue growth, while operating expenses stayed around $12 billion.

Personally, I found Opus’s insight more useful because it challenges a key assumption behind the investment thesis.

My takeaway: Both models found relevant weaknesses, but Opus identified the issue I considered more important for the investment decision.

Fable showed stronger attention to valuation consistency, but I didn’t see a clear overall advantage in this test.

My overall conclusion

I could have pushed these tests further, but I’m not trying to create another benchmark…

I just wanted to know if Fable could improve the stock research I do every week.

Fable reminded me of that smart student who tries so hard to show how smart he is, even when nobody asked. But that could change with new updates.

For now, I don’t see a good reason to switch from Opus 5.5.

And I think there’s a simple reason for my results.

My skills already tell Claude what to research, which numbers matter, and how to challenge my assumptions.

So the model doesn’t have to figure everything out on its own.

Opus already does a great job following that process.

My biggest takeaway? A good research process matters more than always having the smartest AI model.

I’ll keep testing new models.

But for now, I’d rather spend my time improving my Skills than switching models every time a new one comes out.
