I got inside a North Korean hiring scam. What I found reveals a troubling shift in tactics

A screenshot from my Zoom call with “Anthony Lander.”

“Tell me about yourself, about your background?”

That’s how my technical job interview started with a company whose website said it was “Building the future of decentralized finance and blockchain infrastructure.”

They were looking to hire a full stack software engineer, but didn’t seem to care that my LinkedIn profile described me as a journalist who investigates digital deception. And that I have zero engineering experience.

I joined a Zoom call with two men. One, Anthony Lander, said he was based in Miami and spoke Spanish and English. He explained that I’d be asked to do a live coding exercise “to see how you work, how you think, how you approach the real problems.”

The exercise served as the culmination of a sophisticated global hiring scam. As soon as a candidate began working with code, a hidden back door quietly exfiltrated their passwords and crypto wallets. A victim who captured and evaluated the malware described its design to me as “beautiful, simple and clean.”

It was also North Korean, according to an Indicator investigation corroborated by two security experts, the Google Threat Intelligence Group, and analysis of two cryptocurrency wallets by TRM Labs.

The operation provides a rare window into how some North Korean hacking teams behind what the security industry calls Contagious Interview — a years-long campaign that uses fake job offers to deliver malware to tech workers — are moving beyond spray-and-pray link spam into something more elaborate and convincing.

The hackers recruited unwitting freelancers in the Philippines, Nigeria, Colombia, and Bangladesh to put a human face on the scheme. The workers conducted video interviews, managed candidate pipelines, used an internal Slack, and fielded questions from suspicious applicants. One man, who has a day job as a government employee in Bangladesh, was apparently hired to obtain LinkedIn profiles for the operation. The hackers paid them in crypto, provided scripts, and coached them on how to seem more professional. The boss, who used a variety of names and communicated in stilted English, never showed his face or joined a phone conversation.

Nick Carlsen, a senior investigator with TRM Labs and a former FBI intelligence analyst who investigated DPRK hackers, said the operation showcases "the growing sophistication of what North Korea is doing across multiple different groups."

It exemplifies how large-scale digital deception increasingly converges with human exploitation. Investment and romance scams often rely on forced laborers who have been trapped inside compounds in Cambodia and elsewhere. They've been tricked into scamming victims via messaging, social, and dating apps. Russia and Iran use digital platforms to recruit witting and unwitting people in Europe and elsewhere to commit acts of vandalism, sabotage, and, in some cases, espionage. Such incidents sow discord in the West, and provide fodder for online information operations.

North Korea also recruits people to assist with its criminal operations. In the most famous example, DPRK hackers recently stole nearly $300 million via a months-long scheme that included sending real people to a conference and “building profiles, both personal and professional, that could withstand scrutiny during a business or counterparty relationship,” according to a statement from Drift, the crypto company that was targeted.

Recruiting real-world intermediaries is less common in Contagious Interview operations. This hiring scam signals a new, more effective approach that, as Carlsen put it, uses “unwitting or witting foreign facilitators to convince targets of the legitimacy of what they're doing.”

Prior to my job interview, I'd tracked the hiring scam as it transitioned from one fake company to another, using names like Genusix Labs and Keras Labs and impersonating Kuru Labs, a real crypto company. I spoke to six workers and obtained screenshots, documents, code, crypto payment addresses, and internal communications.

I shares materials and code with Marcus Hutchins, the principal threat researcher for Expel who recently published research about a Contagious Interview group.

“I would say it is almost certainly North Korean, as likely as it can be while still leaving a margin of error,” he told me.

The Google Threat Intelligence Group reviewed some of Indicator's findings and also assessed that they were consistent with “a North Korean threat cluster.”

“GTIG identified overlaps with a group we track as UNC5975, which is a financially-motivated North Korean threat cluster that primarily targets blockchain and cryptocurrency-related entities,” said a statement from GTIG spokesperson Mark Karayan.

The hackers used at least one Google account to create documents, including a spreadsheet that tracked which candidates had been interviewed. Other companies whose products were abused by the hackers declined to say whether they saw evidence of DPRK activity, including LinkedIn, Hostinger, and Telegram.

“It's fascinating how elaborate they’re getting with these schemes,” Hutchins said. “I wouldn't have thought hosting a whole interview framework would have boosted infection rate. But they’re hiring a lot of people and investing in a lot of infrastructure.”

Boris Vujičić had his guard up.

He’d recently lost his job at Step Finance, a crypto startup, when it was infiltrated by North Korean hackers who made off with $40 million, forcing the company to shut down.

So Vujičić was wary when he received a DM on LinkedIn from an executive at a web3 company called Genusix Labs. But the person who reached out had a detailed work history, the company had a website, and Genusix’s list of investors included multiple recognized angel investors in the crypto world, along with two legitimate VC firms.

Vujičić agreed to an initial interview and had a brief Zoom call with a young woman who told him that she was based in Singapore. She spoke what he described as perfect English. He proceeded to the next stage, a technical interview.

Vujičić soon joined a Zoom call with two men. One, “Andy Walkerman,” was listed on the Genusix website as a “Team Lead.” The other, “Pedro,” wasn't.

They discussed “technology, some interesting problems I solved etc, after which they gave me a link to a repo with a task,” Vujičić told me in an email. When he expressed wariness about working with an unfamiliar code repository, the interviewers didn't pressure him. They even encouraged him to look for back doors.

Vujičić began working with the code. Soon after, a notification popped up on his Mac: "patch.sh wants to run as a background process." He realized something was wrong and immediately turned off his WiFi. It was too late. The malicious code had exfiltrated over 600 of his saved Chrome passwords, the macOS keychain that could decrypt them, and his MetaMask crypto wallet data. It took just 57 seconds.

Vujičić was shocked that he’d been taken in.

“I’m getting somewhere between 1 and 5 scams like this almost daily on LinkedIn, this one was just more sophisticated than others and I let my guard down,” he said. “This one was different because it had a website, [the] introductory call was with [an'] ‘HR’ person that was listed on a website so it felt real.”

Genusix had a company profile and multiple employee profiles on LinkedIn, along with an X account. The company’s website listed 12 team members, several high-profile investors, and had a GitBook-hosted section with details about its crypto trading platform.

Roughly two weeks after Vujičić was scammed, I used the Calendly link provided by the Genusix employee to sign up for a 30-minute interview. I logged on and was greeted by a young woman who said she worked in HR.

“Can you walk me through your background and what you're currently working on, please?” said the woman, who I’ll call Mary to protect her identity. (I’ll do the same for other workers I spoke to.)

I identified myself as a journalist and told her that I’d spoken to someone who said they’d gone through the hiring process and been infected with malware during the technical interview. I said this appeared to be a hiring scam.

She paused and said, “I think I can tell you everything that you need.”

Mary is a freelancer based in the Philippines who was hired to interview candidates. Her new boss, who went by Javier, gave her a basic script, access to the company Calendly and Zoom accounts, and to Slack. Javier said she’d be paid in crypto based on the number of interviews she conducted. He also told her to tell candidates that she was based in Singapore.

Javier typically communicated in curt messages sent via Telegram and Slack. He often instructed Mary on how to seem more professional during interviews and in emails. In one Slack message he coached her on how to make candidates more interested in the job:

After each call she moved candidates to the next stage, the technical interview. Each applicant was entered into a spreadsheet, which I obtained. It listed dozens of software developers, most of whom were in the Global South or Eastern Europe.

Mary told me that she often interviewed five people per day. By the time we spoke, she’d worked for Genusix for nearly two months and had grown increasingly suspicious. She never saw Javier’s face or heard his voice. At one point, her boss's name on Telegram changed from Javier to Rayhan Prodhan. And the interviews never seemed to result in hires.

“I feel like I'm the only real person here because they never show their faces,” she said.

As Mary’s suspicions rose, Javier told her that they would be hiring for a related company called Keras Labs.

Mary didn’t know it at the time, but Vujičić had publicly shared his experience in an X thread and he’d reported the operation’s technical infrastructure to GitHub, Hostinger, and other service providers. As platforms took down Genusix assets, a new fake company, Keras Labs, emerged. It had a nearly identical website. (The operation also impersonated a legitimate crypto company, Kuru Labs.)

When it shifted from Genusix to Keras, the operation’s fake LinkedIn profiles simply added Keras to their work experience. On April 23, Tushar Ahmed’s LinkedIn profile identified him as the co-founder of Genusix, the general partner of Blockchain Founders Fund, and a former Goldman Sachs analyst. After the Genusix LinkedIn page went offline, the Ahmed profile simply changed its title to “Fractional Chief Staff Officer” at Keras Labs and ditched the affiliation with Blockchain Founders Fund. Now he was a general partner at BlackRock. (LinkedIn declined to say if it removed the Genusix company page or to comment on specific profiles.)

Mary was ready to quit. She began declining interviews, which frustrated Javier. Soon, other low-wage workers appeared in the company Slack.

Javier used a digital avatar for his Slack account profile

I spoke to two Filipino women and a Nigerian man who, like Mary, were hired to interview applicants.

“All I had to do was to interview candidates for the company,” Sheffy, the Nigerian man, told me over the phone. “They were just looking for an English speaker.”

Sheffy was instructed to tell candidates that he was based in the US. At one point his boss, using the same Telegram account that Mary had interacted with, asked Sheffy to reach out to recruiting companies in order to convince them to refer candidates.

“He placed me on the call and he’d tell me to change my name to someone else,” Sheffy said.

Another Slack account belonged to a Bangladeshi man named Kawshar. His Facebook profile said he works as the assistant director in the Department of Youth Development for Panchagarh district.

He was one of several Bangladeshi men inside the Slack. Their role was initially unclear, though I had seen that the Genusix X account was managed by someone in Bangladesh.

The operation’s LinkedIn presence also featured ties to Bangladesh. After it shifted away from Genusix, it added an employee profile: Sujon Pramanik. Unlike the other profiles, which seemed to use fabricated identities, Pramanik appeared to be a freelancer in Bangladesh.

He told me via WhatsApp that he was paid 1,000 BDT (roughly $8 US) to rent his LinkedIn account. Pramanik said he lost the ability to log in soon after agreeing to rent it.

“Any activity on the account is not being carried out by me,” he said. I asked who paid him and he shared a Bangladeshi phone number. I ran it through OSINT Industries and it came up with a clear result: Kawshar, the government worker who was in the Slack.

I reached Kawshar on WhatsApp but he didn’t respond to questions.

Along with Pramanik, the operation added additional accounts to its fake workforce. One was a Singapore-based software engineer named Xu Yue. His profile photo was a composite combining the body and background of a photo from a TED Talk by the late filmmaker Morgan Spurlock with the head of an unidentified Asian man.

LinkedIn provided a statement but didn't respond to specific questions, including why some profiles were removed but others connected to Genusix remained online and were able to transition to Keras Labs.

“Our teams actively focus on detecting state-sponsored abuse on LinkedIn as fraud on the internet is becoming more sophisticated, including through the use of AI,” said Autumn Cobb, a company spokesperson, in an email. “We remove fake accounts based on information from our internal anti-abuse systems and partnerships across the industry. Our policies are clear that every profile on LinkedIn must represent a real person or company and when we discover an account that violates these standards, through our systems or member reports, we take action.”

The company removed 10 accounts associated with the operation after I reached out.

The Javier boss persona communicated via short English messages, paid workers in crypto, never went on camera, and refused phone calls. Its Telegram account was linked to a Colombian phone number, while the company Calendly had a credit card with an American zip code that Hutchins, the malware researcher, told me it was likely stolen. (Calendly did not respond to a request for comment.)

The conflicting signals help obscure the operation’s origins. The job descriptions and other documents I obtained also failed to reveal any useful metadata. The only notable piece of information was that some of the job postings were drafted using a ChatGPT plugin for Microsoft Word.

But there was one telling slip-up. During one of his conversations with Mary, Javier shared a screenshot of an email they’d sent to a candidate. He was trying to show her how to address concerns about the coding exercise. The screenshot revealed that the boss’s email client was set to Chinese:

That’s consistent with North Korean tradecraft, according to Hutchins.

“Their email being set to Chinese is actually a strong signal it is North Korea,” he told me. North Korean hackers often operate out of China or Russia and typically avoid communicating in Korean, he said.

“They try to communicate mostly in English, but internally when communicating with each other they do use Chinese,” Hutchins said.

Then there’s the malware. He reviewed the code contained in the repo that Vujičić had downloaded, as well as screenshots and documents I obtained.

“The Chinese language use, the TTPs [tactics, techniques, and procedures], the crypto, these are all pretty solid tells that these are DPRK,” he said.

Hutchins’ conclusion aligns with findings from the Google Threat Intelligence Group and Kieran Miyamoto, a security researcher who has been tracking Contagious Interview activity on npm, the world's largest software registry. Miyamoto, whose day job is principal cyber threat analyst at a high-profile company, shares his findings at kmsec.uk.

In a post published May 1, Miyamoto detailed how the domain deoft[.]online and email jr11282025[@]proton.me were used as part of recent Contagious Interview activity on npm and Cloudflare. Vujičić had found that the same domain was used in the malware that infected him. The Proton Mail address was connected to Javier’s Slack account.

“This email address was used by a Contagious Interview operator to create an account jason3 on npm and then publish a malicious package that acted as a lynchpin for delivering PylangGhost malware, an infostealer written in Python,” Miyamoto told me in an email.

A final link to DPRK came via the payments made to workers. Tom Caliendo, a cryptocurrency investigator with TRM Labs, analyzed the two crypto addresses used to pay Mary and Sheffy. One was on the Solana blockchain and the other was BNB Smart Chain. He found that the BSC address received more than $3,000 in funds from a crypto wallet that was used as part of a DPRK-linked hiring scam. A victim of that earlier scam said they fell for a fake job application process. The stolen funds were tracked to a wallet owned by the hackers, which in turn sent money to the BSC wallet that was used to pay freelancers like Mary and Sheffy.

“We traced over $3,000 worth of funds that went to the BSC address you gave us,” Caliendo said.

As the tech industry grapples with AI-driven layoffs, job-seeking developers will continue to be easy prey. The ease with which the DPRK hackers can hire freelancers and create or obtain the assets needed to build a convincing fake company only heightens the risk, according to Hutchins.

“They’re from an unreachable country, they’re using fake IDs, they can steal as many GitHub accounts and LinkedIn profiles and passports as humanly possible. There is nothing you can really do to block that level of activity,” he said.

Miyamoto said the operation illustrates the multinational, sophisticated operations of DPRK threat groups.

“We know from earlier reports that Contagious Interview is a highly organized criminal enterprise, but your research showcases how they leverage a multinational supply chain to conduct it, and offers a unique insight into their internal workings,” he told me.

After I spoke to Mary, I used a Calendly link to sign up for a technical interview. I logged into Zoom was greeted by two young men: “Anthony Lander” and “Daniel Murcia.” Lander wore oversized glasses and Murcia had a backwards baseball cap.

Lander asked me to describe my work experience and I explained that I work in journalism and that developers who did the coding exercise had been infected with malware. I said I’m writing a story and wanted to hear from them.

“Ok so let me now contact my leader or my manager because as I recall this was for a full stack interview so I was prepared for that, not for a journalism interview,” he said.

Lander was the same man who'd appeared in Vujičić’s interview, except he’d called himself Pedro. I ran screenshots of both men's faces through reverse image search tools and came up empty. I also paid to run them through the PimEyes and Lenso facial recognition engines. Lander was a high probability match for a Colombian man with a presence on Upwork and other global freelance platforms. His name was Pablo.

Soon, we were chatting on WhatsApp.

“I’m 22 years old, I studied programming, and I’m currently focused on my studies,” he said. “Then someone contacted me on Fiverr and told me they had a job for me— that I’d be working as Anthony Lander and conducting interviews.”

It was a familiar story: the person who hired him never appeared on camera or spoke on the phone. Pablo’s job was to conduct technical interviews. He was paid in crypto. And while he never realized that the coding exercise was a ruse to deliver malware, he’d become suspicious of the whole thing and eventually stopped working for the company.

“My colleagues and I have fallen victim to the lies of Keras Labs or Kuru Labs,” Pablo said. “We conducted interviews and tried to be clear, but we didn't realize we were caught up in something so big.”

  • It started with an X thread posted by Boris Vujičić. He listed the company’s GitHub account, which led to its website. Both were still online. I decided to see how far I could get.

  • The website listed several team members and investors and had links to GitHub, X and LinkedIn.

  • I checked the genusix.com registration and DNS info using DomainTools. It was recently registered and didn’t have any unique hosting, servers, or other infrastructure.

  • I checked the About this account information for the X account and saw that it listed the account’s location as Bangladesh. I used Ubikron to archive pages on the site, GitHub, X, and LinkedIn. I also used the GoFullPage Chrome extension to grab full page screenshots.

  • I found several LinkedIn profiles from company executives, all of which seemed suspicious. The top executives claimed impressive work histories but had a relatively low number of connections. Reverse image searches came up empty for their profile photos, which also suggested these may not be real people. (I ran Indicator's ABC Profile Scan Chrome Skill on many of them.)

  • As noted in the story, some of the Genusix Labs profiles seemed to have been repurposed or purchased/rented. Some of the names of more junior people appeared to belong to marketing/social media freelancers based in Bangladesh. That aligned with the X account’s location.

  • Many of the team photos listed on the website came up empty in reverse image searches. But a couple of them returned hits for workers in the Philippines on marketplace sites like Fiverr.

  • I ran some initial web searches to identify other accounts and content related to the company. This turned up job postings, the LinkedIn post from a developer who said he was almost scammed by the company, and an X thread from a recruiter who said the company tried to get him to refer candidates.

  • At this point, it appeared that the scheme was consistent with some elements of Contagious Interview. But the indicators that pointed towards people in Bangladesh and the Philippines raised doubts about its origins.

  • Vujičić replied to my email and told me more about how he was connected to the company and who he spoke to. He provided a screenshot of the message sent by a Genusix account on LinkedIn, the URL of the malicious GitHub repo, the names of the people who were on his technical interview call, and a technical description of how the malware worked.

  • One key piece of information was that the LinkedIn DM sent to Vujičić included a Calendly link to book an initial 30-minute interview. I signed up with my real name and LinkedIn profile. That’s how I met Mary.

  • She and other sources provided me with documents and screenshots from the internal Slack, conversations with Javier, and other details. This provided emails, names, phone numbers, and profile photos. I pivoted on all of them. A web search for the Proton Mail address used by “Javier” brought up the malware research by Kieran Miyamoto.

  • I used OSINT Industries to check emails and phone numbers, which helped flesh out identities and provide additional points of contact.

  • I repeated the same processes when I was told by a worker that the scam was shifting from Genusix to Keras Labs, and that they were also using the name Kuru Labs.

  • Mary and other sources provided me with the crypto wallets that were used to pay them for their work. I shared them with TRM Labs to assist with analysis.
