Ring's Camera Gets Off Its Dock

I shared a clip earlier this week of Ring's newest camera, which actually flies around and patrols your house all on its own.

Here's what's going on:

  • The Ring Drone Cam, a $249.99 indoor camera, sits in a dock until a Ring camera, sensor, or alarm goes off. Then it takes off, flies to a preset spot, and streams 2K video to your phone. You can also send it out on demand.

  • To set it up, you carry it through the house once. A system called AI Pilot maps a floor plan, suggests patrol points, and steers around obstacles. The camera stays off while it's docked and only records while it's flying.

  • Flights last up to eight minutes, and Ring says the propellers aren't quiet on purpose. It's scheduled to ship in the U.S. on Jan. 27, 2027, if the FCC signs off.

My POV: Ring first announced a camera like this back in 2020, and it never shipped. The team kept adding sensors and compute until the thing got too heavy to fly, and the developers said it was going to cost something like five grand. So they pulled the brain out, put it in the cloud, and now the drone just flies, streams video, and lands. It only stays in the air for up to eight minutes before it has to recharge, which is probably not ideal, but this is V1. It's about six years late, and still cool nonetheless.

Anthropic Wants Claude to Run Your Work Meetings

Via CNBC

Anthropic announced a new lineup of workplace productivity tools, including Claude Meetings, designed to turn office conversations into actual plans, tasks, and finished work.

Here’s a look at what's launching ➡️

  • Claude Meetings: Adds Claude as a visible participant in Google Meet calls. It can answer questions, pull information from connected apps, draft plans, and create timelines while the meeting is happening.

  • Claude Whiteboards: A collaborative space where teams can brainstorm with Claude, organize ideas into timelines and action items, and turn the results into documents or presentations.

  • Claude Sheets: Lets teams create and edit spreadsheets with AI, including formulas, formatting, multiple tabs, and dropdown menus.

  • Claude Data: Connects to business systems like CRMs and data warehouses to generate dashboards, reports, and narrated videos.

  • Interactive Connectors: Bring tools like Adobe, Atlassian, and HubSpot directly into Claude, allowing users to work with connected applications without switching tabs.

The office suite challenge: This is starting to look a lot like Anthropic's answer to Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace. Rather than offering just another chatbot, Anthropic wants Claude to become the place where teams brainstorm, plan, analyze data, and get work done.

“The bigger picture: AI labs are moving aggressively into enterprise software, and the competition is shifting from individual assistants to integrated workplaces. The more AI can access company context and work alongside employees in the tools they already use, the less friction there is to adopting it. The big question is whether companies will keep adding AI to their existing office software or eventually start doing more of their work inside the AI platforms themselves.“ —

New York's AI Safety Law Starts Taking Shape

Via TechCrunch

New York opened a registration portal for major frontier AI developers this week, taking a significant step toward enforcing its new AI safety law. Companies like OpenAI and Anthropic will need to register by Jan. 1, 2027.

What the law requires: The RAISE Act establishes new safety and transparency obligations for large frontier AI developers generating more than $500 million in annual revenue. Companies must:

  • Publish their AI safety frameworks on their websites.

  • Report critical safety incidents within 72 hours.

  • Release public transparency reports about their frontier models.

Who's enforcing it: The state's new Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust (DIGIT) will oversee implementation and compliance. Violations can carry penalties of up to $1 million initially and $3 million for subsequent offenses.

Independent oversight: Gov. Kathy Hochul also launched the New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research at Binghamton University. The center will independently evaluate advanced AI systems and help regulators review the safety frameworks submitted by AI companies.

“Why it matters: While Washington continues debating how to regulate AI, states are moving ahead with their own safety requirements. New York is putting concrete reporting obligations, oversight mechanisms, and financial penalties in place as frontier models become more powerful. The challenge will be ensuring those rules actually improve safety without creating a patchwork of regulations that stifle innovation.“ —
