Is LinkedIn's New "AI Slop" Button Genius or a Trap?

LinkedIn might have just dropped the best social media feature of the year.

The platform added a button in every post's dropdown menu that says "Seems like AI slop," letting users flag those cringey ChatGPT-sounding posts that have been carpet-bombing everyone's feed.

A few details worth knowing:

  • When you flag a post, LinkedIn reduces its reach beyond the poster's network and privately notifies the poster in their analytics that their content is reading as inauthentic.

  • LinkedIn is also retiring its own "enhance your post" AI writer and replacing it with a proofreading tool that "keeps your voice intact."

  • According to AI-detection company Pangram, roughly two-thirds of current LinkedIn posts read as AI-generated. So there's… a target-rich environment for this feature.

My POV: My one concern is that people could weaponize this, such as flagging posts because they disagree with them, don't like the person, or think the writing feels "too polished." There's no actual detector on the backend. The button is a user suggestion, not proof.

But if people use it in the spirit it was intended, this could be one of LinkedIn's smartest product updates in a long time. It's a rare case of a big platform admitting that too much AI content is making its own product worse. That kind of self-awareness is genuinely refreshing.

White House Meets With AI Companies on Model Testing

Via The Information

The White House hosted leading AI companies— including Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Meta— to review a newly completed voluntary framework for testing the cybersecurity capabilities of advanced AI models.

How it works: The meeting focused on a framework to review whether models under development qualify as “covered frontier models.”

  • Under the voluntary program, participating developers could give the government early access to certain frontier models for up to 30 days before making them available to other trusted partners.

  • The goal is to evaluate whether powerful models could discover software vulnerabilities or carry out sophisticated cyberattacks.

  • During the meeting, the Trump administration reportedly told AI developers that it won’t put open-weight AI models through voluntary safety tests.

Important limit: The executive order says the program cannot be used to create a mandatory federal licensing, permitting, or preclearance requirement for developing or releasing new AI models. That matters because the biggest AI labs have been wary of rules that could slow launches or give the government too much control over model releases.

“The bigger picture: The government and AI labs are trying to find a middle ground that serves both sides. Regulators want early visibility into dangerous capabilities before models are widely released, while companies want to avoid a formal approval regime that slows innovation. The result is a familiar AI governance compromise: voluntary cooperation for now while everyone figures out how much trust is enough.“ —

Alibaba Launches Its Most Powerful AI Model Yet

Following a preview last month, Alibaba has now released Qwen3.8-Max, its largest and most capable AI model to date, in another challenge to leading US frontier labs.

Performance claims: Alibaba says its own testing shows Qwen3.8-Max broadly matching, and sometimes exceeding, Claude Fable 5 on benchmarks. On Arena’s text model leaderboard, it reportedly trails only Fable 5 and three Claude Opus models. For frontend coding, it trails two Claude Opus models and Moonshot’s Kimi K3, while visual analysis remains an area where Fable 5 leads.

China’s open push: The launch adds to a growing wave of powerful Chinese models being made widely available, including Kimi K3. That stands in contrast to many US frontier labs, which have largely kept their strongest models behind closed APIs.

“The bigger picture: The US-China AI race is not cooling down. Chinese labs are moving quickly, releasing highly capable models into the open, and putting pressure on US companies that still dominate the frontier conversation. The next phase of competition may not just be about who has the strongest model, but who can make capable AI cheaper, more accessible, and easier for developers to build on.“ —
