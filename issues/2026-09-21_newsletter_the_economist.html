Today’s top stories

▸ Friedrich Merz called regional elections in Germany “a disaster” for his Christian Democrat Union party, but promised to stay on as the country’s chancellor. Preliminary results suggest the CDU won just 4.9% of the vote in the north-east region of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, its worst-ever result— one that would exclude it from the state’s parliament. The populist-right Alternative for Germany came first with over 38%. In Berlin the populist-left Die Linke booted the CDU from office.

▸ Scott Bessent, America’s treasury secretary, said that his talks with He Lifeng, China’s economy tsar, had been “very successful”. The pair met at the offices of JPMorgan Chase in New York ahead of a summit between the two countries’ leaders in Washington on Thursday. Mr Bessent said that they had agreed to continue discussions about “common goals and common threats” related to AI.

▸ Donald Trumptold Fox News he was open to meeting Iran’spresident, Masoud Pezeshkian, at the UN General Assembly in New York— but still threatened to “blow the entire nation up”. Iran vowed to retaliate against any attack. Over the weekend the Iran-backedHouthis hit Riyadh, Saudi Arabia’s capital. America’s State Department advised its citizens to “reconsider” Middle East travel, warning the regional conflict could “escalate rapidly”.

▸ The average price for a gallon ofdiesel in America rose above $6.50 for the first time, according to the American Automobile Association. The Iran war and Russia’s export controls, which were put in place in response to Ukrainian attacks on its refineries, have made the fuel more expensive. Because diesel powers lorries, trains and power generators, the high price will probably push up the cost of other goods.

▸ Polls closed in Russia’ssham parliamentary elections in which anti-war candidates were banned. Earlier, Ukraine launched a massive drone attack on Moscow oblast, killing at least two people. The governor of the region said 450 drones were shot down. Russian authorities also claimed that voting systems in Moscow faced cyberattacks. Meanwhile, Ukraine said at least four people were killed in a Russian drone strike on the Kyiv region.

▸ Volvo Cars appointed Klaus Zellmer, the boss of Skoda, as its new chief executive. Mr Zellmer, who is expected to start next year, will succeed Hakan Samuelsson, who came out of retirement to try to turn around the struggling Chinese-owned carmaker. Last week Volvo said it would launch 13 new models by 2030 and embark on a cost-cutting programme.

▸ Mr Trump said he would change plans for a monumental arch in Washington, DC to allow space for military personnel and hardware, including snipers and drones. America’s president claimed the changes came at the request of the armed forces; he said the facility would now be a “top grade Military Complex/Triumphal Arch”. Protesters call the arch an ego project.

The day ahead

AFP

The world’s greatest gabfest in New York

Global leaders gather in the Big Apple this week for the United Nations General Assembly. Expect them to focus on global conflicts, rising fuel prices, AI risks— and the future of the UN itself, which has survived a recent funding crisis but is still under pressure from America to reform.

Donald Trump has eased some crises since last year’s UNGA, most recently through his deal with Denmark over Greenland. But the war in Gaza remains unsolved and the one in Ukraine is escalating. Meanwhile, America’s conflict against Iran, which created energy shortages, risks flaring up again.

America refused visas for members of the internationally recognised Palestinian Authority, but granted them for Israel’s prime minister, Binyamin Netanyahu. Mr Trump will meet Britain’s new prime minister, Andy Burnham, and Venezuela’s leader, Delcy Rodriguez. Xi Jinping of China is skipping UNGA altogether. Instead, he will hold talks with Mr Trump at the White House on Thursday. Theirs may be the summit that really matters.

REUTERS

Novo trims the fat

Novo updates investors on its business at its capital-markets day in London on Monday. The Danish drugmaker has recently ceded ground in the lucrative weight-loss market to Eli Lilly, its American rival. Over the past year Novo’s share price has fallen by a quarter, while Lilly’s has risen one-and-a-half-fold. An attempt to diversify beyond treatments for diabetes and obesity has also stumbled. This month Novo halted further trials of an experimental cardiovascular drug after disappointing results.

On September 14th the company dropped “Nordisk” from its name, a rebrand meant to signal a broader cultural overhaul. It also announced a partnership with Anthropic, an AI lab, to speed up drug discovery. But success in obesity remains what matters most. Although Novo still leads in oral weight-loss pills, a small but fast-growing slice of the market, Lilly’s broader pipeline of obesity drugs has fared better in clinical trials. Investors will want to know how Novo plans to regain momentum.

A big moment for the digital euro

On Monday the European Central Bank will switch on Pontes, its new system for making digital euros available to banks by acting as a link between blockchain markets, which hold financial assets as digital tokens, and TARGET services, ie standard European payment systems. Clearstream, which handles post-trade clearing and settlement processes, and Axiology, another capital-markets infrastructure provider, have reportedly signed up to support the project.

Pontes is part of the ECB’sshort-term effort to safely bring tokenisation into European financial markets, alongside a longer-term solution called Appia, which is supposed to work at a global level. Germany’s banking association argues that the ECB ’s centralised system will be unnecessarily complex and inefficient. Many in the digital-currency world would prefer a solution where central-bank money connects directly with blockchains rather than using a bridge mechanism such as Pontes. Still, Christine Lagarde, the ECB’s president, has said the system will allow banks to operate “faster and without friction”.

Seoul searching for cash

The flimsy “reciprocal” trade deals Donald Trump has signed with countries from El Salvador to Indonesia have become a source of consternation among America’s allies. In the year since South Korea signed a pact to invest $350bn in America, it has announced fewer concrete proposals than its neighbour, Japan, to the chagrin of the Trump administration. Relations are tense, particularly after Mr Trump scaled back joint military exercises in August. Last week South Korea’s president said he would not deploy troops towards Mr Trump’s war in Iran.

But South Korea is trying to put the affair to rest. This week it is expected to sign a memorandum of understanding with America, setting out where it will invest $200bn, on top of the $150bn it has committed to shipbuilding. South Korea’s stock of investments was just $96bn last year, compared with the $827bn that Japan invested in America. In parallel, South Korea is investing heavily at home, including $500bn earmarked for its chip sector over the next decade or so. It will take some creative accounting to meet its pledges.
