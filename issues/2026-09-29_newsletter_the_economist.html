Today’s top stories

▸ OpenAIcancelled the release of GPT-6.1 Astra, planned for October, over safety concerns. The new model was not good enough at “staying within scope and authorisation” or communicating about “the type of work it’s done”, the company said. Separately, its rival, Anthropic, devoted roughly a third of its IPO prospectus to warnings of the “existential risks to humanity” of its technology, ahead of its listing, which is expected later this year and could be the largest ever.

▸ Nvidia, the world’s most valuable company, launched a platform to stop AI agents going awry. The chipmaker said the technology could have prevented a security breach at Hugging Face, a developers’ platform that Nvidia agreed to buy this month. Nvidia’s board also approved an additional $150bn to buy back its own shares. That would take the available total to $235bn, the largest buyback scheme in American corporate history.

▸ AMD, a chipmaker, said it was buying World Labs,a startup founded by Fei-Fei Li, a computer scientist, which builds software to help AI make sense of physical spaces. The firms aim to finalise the all-stock, $8.2bn deal by the end of the year. The expertise that AMD will acquire through the deal will, it hopes, help it challenge Nvidia for dominance of the AI-chip market.

▸ A daytime Russian drone strike on the National Academy of Sciences in central Kyiv killed at least two people. Seven more were killed and a total of 80 injured in attacks throughout Ukraine, according to Volodymyr Zelensky, the country’s president. Russia has begun using faster, high-flying jet drones; Mr Zelensky said his forces could intercept only about half of them.

▸ South Korea’s government denied delaying an investigation into mine explosions in its Demilitarised Zone that injured three soldiers last week. The armed forces said they suspected North Korea of planting the devices. But the country’s main opposition party accused the government of procrastinating, potentially giving the North time to dispose of evidence, to ease the countries’ relations.

▸ Christine Lagarde, president of the European Central Bank, suggested that rising bond yields could reduce the need for the ECB to raise interest rates. The bank has increased its key rate twice this year to combat inflation. Even if it holds steady when it next meets at the end of October, investors expect to see more rate rises this year.

▸ SpaceX’s Starship rocket reached orbit for the first time, despite an engine failure that had briefly led the firm to say it would not attempt the feat. The rocket’s upper stage began deploying 26 new Starlink satellites. Starship can carry bigger, better satellites than Falcon 9, its predecessor. That makes it central to SpaceX’s expansion plans, and its $2trn valuation.

The day ahead

AP

Malaysia begins repatriating Rohingya

For nearly a decade the Rohingya, a Muslim minority in Myanmar, have fled persecution on perilous boat journeys to Malaysia. Life there has never been easy for the more than 100,000 refugees. Conditions are about to get harder still. Anti-Rohingya sentiment, whipped up on social media, is rising. Anwar Ibrahim, Malaysia’s prime minister, appears to have bowed to it. He has announced a plan to send the Rohingya home; the first set of roughly 1,500 left on Tuesday.

Mr Anwar insists that Myanmar’s junta has agreed to take them back. But more than 160 civil-society groups warn that the returnees face violence and forced conscription amid a brutal civil war. The bigger worry is how Bangladesh will interpret the move. Bangladeshis are growing weary of the 1.2m Rohingya seeking refuge in their country. Malaysia’s example may tempt them to rush plans of their own. The crisis facing the Rohingya, already considered the world’s most persecuted people, threatens to deepen further.

America’s diesel time-bomb continues to tick

Just weeks before the midterm elections, diesel prices are hovering near a record $6.50 a gallon, squeezing farmers and truckers. Donald Trump thinks he has a solution. On September 22nd he said he wanted to ban diesel exports. The White House has since denied that a ban is imminent. But traders are betting that some restrictions may still materialise. American wholesale diesel is trading at a widening discount to European prices as markets price in the prospect of more fuel being kept at home.

American diesel may become dearer again. Brent crude has climbed above $107 a barrel, pushing up the cost of diesel. A ban could also backfire. Because refineries produce diesel, petrol and jet fuel from the same barrel of crude, a diesel export ban would quickly fill storage tanks— forcing refiners to slow production and reduce supplies of all three fuels. Unfortunately for Mr Trump, there is no easy way out of America’s diesel crunch.

REUTERS

Trump’s Golden Dome faces a reality check

On Tuesday a group of American senators will scrutinise President Donald Trump’s “Golden Dome” initiative, his plan to shield America from missiles. Inspired by Israel’s Iron Dome, the project will be monumental for a country 450 times larger. Since Mr Trump unveiled the plan in May 2025, worries have mounted about its cost and technical feasibility.

America already has interceptors designed to protect it against minor attacks from countries such as North Korea. Defending against the much larger arsenals of China and Russia would require something far more elaborate, including space-based sensors and weapons. The more comprehensive the shield, the more expensive it becomes. Congress made a down-payment of $24bn last year, but estimates for the complete system range from $175bn to the low trillions. Defence contractors spy a golden goose. As the war in Ukraine has demonstrated, stopping missiles costs far more than firing them.

Evan Gershkovich recounts life behind Russian bars

Evan Gershkovich, an American journalist, spent 16 months in prison in Russia. He chronicles the ordeal in a moving memoir, “This Cursed Beautiful Land”, published on Tuesday. The book recounts his seizure in 2023 by balaclava-clad agents of the FSB, Russia’s main security service, and his experience behind bars: the routines, indignities and snatched joys. Soon after a sham trial on espionage charges, he was freed in 2024 in an exchange of prisoners, among them a Russian assassin incarcerated in Germany.

Mr Gershkovich replays the risks and decisions that led to his arrest, pondering whether the FSB — and Russia’s president, Vladimir Putin— really believed he was a spy. He retraces the diplomatic efforts that led to his release. But at the heart of the book is the friendship he formed with his cellmate in Lefortovo, a notorious jail in Moscow. This tale of punishment without a crime is also a testimony to human connection.
