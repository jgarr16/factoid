The perils of AI image editing and detection

The US midterms offered a case study in the lack of AI literacy among professionals who should know better.

On election night, Free Press reporter Olivia Reingold shared a photo she said was from a Democratic gathering in Michigan:

A style writer at Vanity Fair took a closer look at the image and noticed that several details looked off. They wrote:

“The image has several discrepancies. The style and make of the Chanel Flap bag is inconsistent with others the brand has sold, and the Valentino shoes in question appear askew in proportion; their straps have several punched holes that the authentic Valentino style does not.“ — Vanity Fair

A fellow Vanity Fair writer tweeted, “AI image detectors confirmed that yes, Olivia Reingold used an AI-generated image to make her point, without a drop of disclosure. Wild!!!!”

She added: “Thank you @pangram and @GoogleDeepMind SynthID for your attention to this matter.”

Pangram’s new image detector (which we also highlight below) and the SynthID detector offered these results for the image:

That was enough for critics to accuse Reingold of presenting an AI-generated image as real, a significant ethical breach.

But the truth was more nuanced — and a good illustration of the lack of AI literacy.

Reingold explained that she “used ChatGPT to brighten and sharpen an image of a woman in Valentino shoes and a Chanel bag. After processing, it still shows these same things: a woman wearing Valentino shoes and a Chanel bag.”

Not exactly. Look at Reingold’s original and the edited version:

Olivia Reingold via X

ChatGPT did more than just “brighten and sharpen” the image. It distorted it. Simply put, a journalist should not use ChatGPT as an image editing tool. As Aric Toler of the New York Times pointed out:

“Ignoring all the political stuff, it is interesting/depressing how even when you just do what seems like "harmless" image editing with an AI tool, it gives the new image an unmistakable AI sheen to skin and the background. And, of course, it changes the actual objects themselves.“ — Aric Toler

It’s a problem that Reingold didn’t know this and a problem that she didn’t disclose her use of ChatGPT in the original tweet.

But along with Reingold’s ethical lapse, others failed to account for the nuances (and limits) of AI detection tools.

The SynthID detector result said that “This media was made or edited with OpenAI.” Pangram flagged the image as “AI-generated or edited with AI tools.” Many people assume such verdicts mean that an image was fully created with AI. That’s not necessarily the case.

The image Reingold shared wasn’t a camera original. But is it accurate to label it “AI-generated,” as some did? Does calling it “edited with AI” undersell how much it changed? (Then there’s the fact that the latest phone cameras also use AI, which is a whole other issue...)

Expect more confusion and more examples like this. People will reach for AI tools when they shouldn’t, and others will engage in the synthetic media gotcha game without accounting for the nuance and limits of detection tools. — Craig

Tools & Tips

Lots of OSINT tools disappear, get shut down, or stop working. It’s rare to see one come back from the dead.

I recently noticed SpyOnWeb was back online after a hiatus. It used to be one of my go-to tools to search for websites that share the same Google Analytics or AdSense ID. (If you’re not familiar with the technique, check out my guide to connecting websites together using OSINT tools and techniques. Or watch the workshop video.)

SpyOnWeb went offline around the fall of 2024. But it’s back on a new domain — spyonweb.net instead of .com — and offers many of the original features, plus new ones. You can search for domains that share the same AdSense/Analytics ID, research nameservers and historic IPs, track three domains or IPs for free, and more.

David Carrero Fernández-Baillo, the owner, told me in an email that the new site “isn't a continuation of the original spyonweb.com, and it's not the same owner. I was a regular user of the original service, and when the .com stopped working I missed having the tool, so I decided to build a new version.”

The service scans domains and extracts data including Google IDs (UA-, G-, GTM, AW-, pub-), IP addresses, nameservers, the SSL certificate, and more.

He said the data goes back to September 2025. (Services like BuiltWith and DNSlytics will likely have better historical data.)

Carrero said that if you search a domain that’s not in the database, the system queues it for a future run. “So check again a little later,” he said.

Along with three free site monitors, you can run 10 queries per hour without being logged in. — Craig

Reports & Research

Can AI Scale Human Fact-Checking? Evidence from Community Notes

📍 A new preprint by Thomas Renault, Mohsen Mosleh, and David Rand found that AI-generated Community Notes on X “are submitted more quickly, are longer, and cite more and higher-quality sources than human-written notes.” They were also rated helpful at a similar rate and were linked to similar drops in reposts once published. AI-written notes were twice as likely as human ones to cite fact-checking websites.

📍 Wired and AI Forensics found that a network of more than 400 innocent-looking AI apps and websites was secretly offering tools to create sexually abusive deepfakes. Wired wrote that the apps “appear to be run by Chinese developers and a Hong Kong-registered entity called KeLing Technology Limited.”

📍 Bill Adair’s Grokipedia page used to confuse him with a mid-century baseball player. Still, Adair argues the AI encyclopedia has some strengths that its rival Wikipedia could learn from.

📍 Speaking of Wikipedia, and specifically its notability policy: Wired looked at the “tragedy” of having your page deleted for being insufficiently significant. And a marketing consultant considered whether the policy makes the platform biased toward incumbents in US elections.

📍 Jeff Kosseff, of “The Twenty-Six Words That Created the Internet” fame, makes the case that the court fight between Elon Musk and Minnesota over the state’s law imposing fines on providers of AI nudifiers may define how liable AI companies can be held for harmful content more broadly.

📍 The Wesleyan Media Project concluded last week that spending on AI ads during this US election cycle “surpassed $187 million,” with just over 90% of spend coming from Republicans. WMP notes that “our calculation of spending is almost surely lower than the actual level of spending given that many ads do not contain disclaimers and some instances of AI use are difficult to determine with the human eye alone.”
