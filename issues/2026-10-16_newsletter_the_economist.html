Today’s top stories

▸ Volodymyr Zelenskyis hoping to rally support from European leaders at a summit in Brussels on Friday, as Donald Trump threatens to cripple the Ukrainian war effort by buying Russian diesel. Ukraine’s president said that his country would not acquiesce to Mr Trump’s demand that it stop attacking Russian oil refineries. That angered America’s president, who said new Ukrainian strikes were a “disgrace”.

▸ In an interview with The Economist,Alice Weidel, the co-leader of the hard-right Alternative for Germanyparty, said her country should withdraw its support for Ukraine. Instead, she argued, Germany should pressure Mr Zelensky to end the war before Russian hybrid attacks escalate. The AfD is on the cusp of running its first state government in Saxony-Anhalt.

▸ The Houthis, an Iran-backed militia, attacked Al-Maashiq palace, the headquarters of Yemen’s government in the city of Aden. At least five people were killed and 16 injured, including security guards for Shayea al-Zindani, Yemen’s prime minister. Saudi Arabia accused Iran of encouraging the Houthis to escalate in Yemen. Recently the Houthis attacked the airport in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia’s capital, and the headquarters of Saudi Aramco, the state oil concern.

▸ Investors continued to fret about AI-related firms’ debt. Shares in SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics, two South Korean chipmakers, tumbled by 10% on Thursday, though they recovered slightly on Friday. Several big American chip stocks also fell amid a two-week sell-off. The spread on CoreWeave’s credit-default swaps, a type of insurance for bondholders, rose by 16% from a week ago. Its stock fell, along with that of other neoclouds.

▸ The Pentagon cancelled plans to livestream the execution of Nidal Malik Hasan, who killed 13 American troops in 2009. Mr Trump expressed regrets that the world would not see “how America deals with Terrorists and SCUM” but said that “many very good and well meaning people would rather not watch him die.” Recent polling showed that 80% of Americans opposed the broadcast.

▸ Metareleased a new open-weight AI model, Muse Spark Open. Meta partnered with Apple so that it would be optimised for advanced Mac computers. The technology giants said this would reduce developers’ reliance on expensive cloud-computing providers. Demand for open-weight models, which developers can run on their own servers and customise, is soaring. The most advanced ones typically come from China.

▸ The European Central Bank gave UniCredit permission to take over Commerzbank. In July UniCredit acquired a 48% stake in the German bank, but needed the ECB’s approval to proceed. The Italian lender said that it expected to take control of Commerzbank by the end of the year, once it had completed the “remaining regulatory processes and conditions”.

The day ahead

GETTY IMAGES

America and Ukraine look increasingly like frenemies

Volodymyr Zelensky has had few weeks so rotten as this one. After striking a scandalous deal with Vladimir Putin to buy Russian diesel, Donald Trump is using American arms and intelligence as leverage to stop Ukraine attacking Russian refineries. Russia, meanwhile, is largely ignoring the “energy ceasefire” Mr Trump announced. In Ukraine, Russian missiles are now arriving without the usual warning from Uncle Sam. And Elon Musk’s Starlink internet, on which many Ukrainian drones rely, has been throttled (Mr Musk blames the American government).

Ukraine has resumed hitting Russian refineries. That may be rash— upsetting Mr Trump may drive the wedge deeper with America, on which Ukraine relies for 95% of its Patriot interceptors. Ukraine could instead increase attacks on other Russian targets, including data centres and distribution hubs. And the White House has suggested that Mr Trump’s energy truce covers only refineries— of which Ukraine has just one. Mr Zelensky could appeal to Republicans in Congress about this injustice. Above all, he must keep a cool head.

ALAMY

Setting the economic world to rights

Kevin Warsh was tight-lipped during a discussion with the International Monetary Fund’s managing director on Friday morning. At the Federal Reserve chair’s “fireside chat” with Kristalina Georgieva at the IMF’s autumn meeting in Bangkok, he offered his first public remarks since America reported far higher inflation than expected for September. They will also be his last before the Fed’s next rate decision on October 28th. Anyone looking for a hint to where the Fed’s benchmark interest rate is going was disappointed. “We’re not in that business anymore,” he said.

Mr Warsh did, though, welcome America’s AI-investment boom, which recently prompted the IMF to revise up its forecasts for growth (and inflation) in the country. He said the technology would bring “a huge benefit to the global economy” but that it would be a “bumpy” path. Stocks linked to AI, which Mr Warsh perhaps jokingly called “superintelligence”, saw a dramatic sell-off on Thursday because of worries about borrowing underpinning AI infrastructure.

AP

European exporters hold their own

European leaders gathered on Thursday and discussed global imbalances in trade— by which they mean the bloc’s yawning deficit with China. But for all the political focus on China, it is important for policymakers not to lose sight of the broader picture. On Friday, trade figures for August will be published that provide details on how the EU is trading with the rest of the world, too.

Looking at trade through a wider lens usually shows that the EU is holding up well. Imports to the EU are rising faster than its exports. But that is partly because of stronger demand within the bloc itself— which is no sign of weakness— and partly because Europe is an energy importer, and energy is expensive at present. In manufacturing, the EU runs a healthy surplus of about €30bn a month with the rest of the world. Chemicals alone account for about €20bn of that. Chinese competition may be fierce, but Europe’s exporters, in some sectors at least, can still hold their own.

SUPPLIED BY LMK

Elizabeth Holmes continues to confuse

“You Can See Everything”, released on Friday, is a documentary about Elizabeth Holmes, who was sentenced to more than 11 years for fraud and conspiracy relating to Theranos, her blood-testing startup. She let the cameras into her home in San Diego for 34 days before she reported to prison in May 2023.

The co-director, Nathan Fielder, a Canadian film-maker and comedian, spends the first half of the film quizzing Ms Holmes and her partner on the charges. She squirms and obfuscates, insisting that she has never told a lie in her adult life. Baffled, Mr Fielder enlists the help of others who were sucked into Ms Holmes’s orbit.

John Carreyrou, the reporter who partly uncovered Ms Holmes’s fraud, has described her as having “a reality distortion field” that “forced people to momentarily suspend disbelief”. Indeed, a subject who willingly throws open her door would seem to have nothing to hide. But is her openness a disguise in itself? The audience is kept guessing— and glued to the screen.
