Today’s top stories

▸ Donald Trump said every American adult would be paid $5,000 if the Republicans “win” November’s midterm elections. The president, speaking on the first day of the Republicans’ mini-convention, gave scant details of his plan but told voters to imagine he was on the ballot. Two problems: the offer might well be illegal; and Mr Trump’s popularity is tanking— The Economist’s tracker has his net approval at -26.

▸ America’s Treasury departmentsaid it would begin to buy back up to $6bn-worth of long-dated government debt on Thursday as it attempts to contain borrowing costs. But investors, expecting a bigger purchase programme, were disappointed: yields on ten-year Treasuries briefly rose to their highest level since 2023. The Treasury had committed to “at least double” buybacks to $4bn when it announced the scheme last month.

▸ Brent crude, the international oilbenchmark, surpassed $100 a barrel for the first time since July, as traders reacted to strikes between America and Iran. Iran said it had launched missiles at ten ships and an American base in Jordan in retaliation for American attacks that destroyed five tankers. Mr Trump claimed the war would end “immediately after” the midterms.

▸ John Ternus,Apple’snew boss, unveiled “Duo”, the company’s first foldable iPhone. It costs $1,999 and goes on sale next month. Rivals have long offered similar gadgets, although they have hardly flown off the shelves. Mr Ternus positioned Apple’s version as an improvement, saying existing foldables felt like “two phones awkwardly stuck together”. Apple also announced price hikes on new iPhone 18 models.

▸ A criminal trial of Huawei began in New York. Prosecutors from the Department of Justice accuse the Chinese tech giant of various crimes, including stealing technology from American companies and abusing America’s financial system as part of an “effort to dominate the telecommunications industry around the world”. Huawei’s legal team criticised the government for “cherry-picking isolated events”.

▸ Five people died after a ferry caught fire in the Philippines. Eighty-six more are reported missing. The blaze broke out on the MV June Aster as it was nearing Coron, a popular tourist destination in Palawan province. The country’s coastguard said around 40 people had been rescued.

▸ London’s police force launched a criminal investigation into Reform UKover alleged foreign funding. Last week two close advisers to Nigel Farage, the party’s leader, resigned after they were filmed discussing how to receive money from an American donor, which would be illegal under British electoral law. Reform UK denies wrongdoing.

The day ahead

DAVID SIMONS

Europe gets set for higher interest rates

The European Central Bank will probably raise interest rates by a quarter of a percentage point on Thursday, to 2.5%. Hopes that the Iran war’s effects on energy prices would be short-lived have been quashed; Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, topped $100 per barrel again on Wednesday after fighting escalated. The ECB will also take note that the European economy has proven more resilient than many expected.

Surveys of purchasing managers show that business activity grew at its highest rate of the year so far in July and August. Oxford Economics, a research firm, forecasts GDP growth of 1.4% in the euro zone in 2027, a decent figure by recent standards. Even Germany, the continent’s sick man, is showing signs of recovery. The Ifo index, a widely watched indicator of business confidence, jumped markedly in August. The combination of price shocks and steady growth will probably keep rates higher in Europe for a while.

ALAMY

A data-centre backlash for Oracle

Oracle, a big American tech company, is trying to convince investors of the wisdom of its data-centre spending spree. When it reports quarterly results on Thursday, analysts expect revenues to rise by 28% compared with the prior year, driven by a more than doubling of cloud-infrastructure sales. But in June the company said its capital spending would reach around $70bn in the fiscal year (to May 2027), higher than many people had expected. The outlay could shrink profits. The latest results will probably show operating margins dipping to 40%, from nearly 42% in the same quarter the previous year.

The firm’s share price has fallen sharply. Investors fret about rising data-centre component costs, growing competition from the likes of SpaceX, which also rents out AI chips, and over-reliance on OpenAI as a customer. Oracle has sound legacy enterprise-software and database operations. But their share of the business is shrinking as Oracle places its biggest bets on AI.

Pity America’s estate agents

America’s housing market is stuck. Last year sales of existing homes sat near their lowest level in three decades, according to the National Association of Realtors, a trade association. The severity of the slump will probably be laid bare again when the NAR publishes figures for August on Thursday. Last autumn the NAR forecast a 14% rise in existing-home sales in 2026; it now expects a modest 4% increase.

In truth, even that projection looks optimistic. Government profligacy, war in the Gulf and persistent inflation have rattled bond markets, raising mortgage costs. At the end of last year, nearly seven in ten mortgages had locked in interest rates at or below 5%; more than half of those were at 4% or lower. In contrast, average rates on 30-year fixed mortgages are currently around 6.7%. With homeowners reluctant to give up such good deals, America’s housing market is unlikely to get moving soon.

The wonders of the Bayeux tapestry

The Bayeux tapestry goes on public display at the British Museum on Thursday. Some 1m people are expected to visit the 70-metre-long, 950-year-old depiction of the Norman invasion of England in 1066. The linen (really not a tapestry but an embroidery, as it was stitched by hand) teems with 737 animals and 627 people, and tells the story of William the Conqueror’s defeat of King Harold at Hastings. The loan from France is a diplomatic coup, forged by Emmanuel Macron, France’s president, to strengthen Anglo-French ties.

Those lucky enough to see it should look closely: you can spot where restorers probably changed some scenes, by putting an arrow in Harold’s eye, for instance. (Our interactive article uncovers some of the tapestry’s mysteries.) Hopeful visitors who miss out on tickets can still see it in Bayeux, Normandy, where it returns to a freshly restored museum next year. No doubt many are already plotting their own Norman invasion.
