Today’s top stories

▸ Houthirebels seized Mocha, a strategic port in western Yemen, as fighting with Saudi-backed forces intensified. Its capture gives the Iran-backed militia greater leverage over the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which connects the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden. The waterway has become particularly important for oilexports since the war with Iran disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.

▸ The sell-off of American government debtcontinued apace, with yields on ten-year bonds reaching 4.96%, approaching their highest since 2007. Investors are fretting about rising oil prices— Brent crude hit $109 per barrel before dropping back— as well as a buyback scheme by the Treasury that was weedier than expected. Worries spread to bond markets elsewhere; benchmark yields surged in Australia and Japan.

▸ Israelsaid it blew up a sprawling Hezbollahtunnel system in southern Lebanon. The underground network is located below Ali al-Taher ridge, near the countries’ border, that Israel has independently designated a buffer zone. Last week Israel seized the area. Iran had previously warned that it would respond forcefully to any Israeli offensive on the ridge.

▸ Anthropicsaid it disrupted several attempts by “threat actors” to misuse Claude, its AI chatbot. The lab said it caught scientists using its tools to research bioweapons. It also said users in China, Russia and Yemen tried to access its models to develop software for weapons and distribute propaganda. This week a former Anthropic researcher accused AI firms of “gambling with our lives”.

▸ An appeals court in Boston rejected Donald Trump’s plans to tighten postal-voting requirements before the midterm elections in November, confirming a lower court’s injunction against the president’s executive order. Mr Trump sought to instate more stringent rules across America for voter registration and ballot design. The Supreme Court is mulling a similar request from the administration.

▸ Oraclereported first-quarter revenues of $19.3bn, up 30% year on year largely thanks to continued demand for its cloud services. Sales in that division rose to $7.4bn, up by 121% from the same period last year. The results boosted investors’ optimism that the American tech firm’s bets on data centres would pay off; its shares rose by 6% in after-market trading.

▸ Two organisers of a long-held vigil to commemorate the Tiananmen Square massacre were sentenced to seven years’ imprisonment in Hong Kong. Lee Cheuk-yan and Chow Hang-tung were convicted under the territory’s national-security law of inciting others to subvert state power. Hong Kong was once the rare place in China where the killing of pro-democracy students in 1989 was memorialised. That changed when China imposed the national-security law in 2020.

The choice facing the Federal Reserve

Kevin Warsh sounds increasingly hawkish. Speaking at a central-bankers’ jamboree in Jackson Hole on August 28th, the chair of the Federal Reserve said that inflation was still too high. Meanwhile, he noted that the economy appeared to have strengthened, and that the labour market remained “consistent with full employment”. Recent data support his case. August payrolls blew past expectations, unemployment remained low at 4.1% and estimates point to annualised GDP growth of 4.7% in the third quarter.

More data arrives on Friday, when America releases consumer-price figures for August. Economists expect headline inflation of 3.4% year on year, the same as in July, and core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy prices, of 2.4%. Markets put the odds of the Fed raising interest rates at its meeting next week at more than 60%. With Donald Trump demanding lower rates, Mr Warsh may have to choose between pleasing the president and preserving his inflation-fighting credibility.

A petrol problem for Russia’s economy

Russia’s central bank has cut interest rates at its past ten meetings, from 21% in mid-2025 to 14% in July. On Friday, however, rate-setters will probably pause: 18 of 19 analysts polled by TASS, a state news agency, expect them to keep rates steady. Petrol explains why. Ukrainian drone strikes on refineries have caused shortages; pump prices in July were 26% higher than a year earlier. Meanwhile, the annual inflation rate is 6%. Although it has fallen since last spring, it remains stubbornly above the bank’s 4% target.

The government continues to splurge on its war in Ukraine. The fiscal stimulus means that demand is running up against severe supply constraints. One is labour: unemployment is close to 2%, nearly an all-time low. But the central bank, which expects the economy to grow by just 1% this year, may be unwilling to raise interest rates soon. Russians can expect little relief from rising prices.

The day the world changed

America mourns those killed in the 9/11 attacks on Friday. Donald Trump will attend a ceremony at the Pentagon to mark 25 years since the atrocity; services will also take place in Manhattan and Somerset County, Pennsylvania, where one of the hijacked planes crashed.

Patriot Day, as September 11th is now known, unites America. But the country’s response to the attacks still causes division: only 48% of Americans think the wars and surveillance that followed were worth the cost. To the dismay of many, the extensive counter-terror apparatus enacted after 9/11 remains largely in place.

Mr Trump’s use of these tools is fuelling debate about whether to curb them. The president has called drug smugglers terrorists to justify strikes on boats in the Caribbean and Pacific. He has also used the Department for Homeland Security, established to fight terrorism, to round up illegal immigrants. Eventually a younger congressional intake, with hazier memories of the attacks, may seek to limit the executive’s powers.

Athletics tries to innovate, again

Athletics stars have long complained that their sport struggles for attention outside the Olympics or World Championships. Last year Michael Johnson, a retired American sprinter with four Olympic golds, tried to fill the void by launching Grand Slam Track, a competition that promised a fan-friendly format and bumper salaries for athletes. It filed for bankruptcy in a matter of months. Now the sport’s governing body, World Athletics, is proposing another solution. Ultimate Championship, which begins on Friday in Budapest, is a three-day event with a $10m prize pot.

The new event comes with gimmicks. Broadcasts will feature 3D -visualisations of sprint finishes; the field area will be coloured black rather than the traditional green. Still, the promise of cash ($150,000 for winning an event) has helped to attract big stars. Britain’s Josh Kerr (pictured) and Norway’s Jakob Ingebrigtsen, for instance, will renew their fierce rivalry in the men’s 1500m.
