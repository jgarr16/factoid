Microsoft joins the decision model race: The tech giant just launched Microsoft-Decision-1, building on Alibaba's Qwen model with a 32K token context window. The model scores a fixed set of options and assigns each a calibrated probability. Microsoft claims it beat a 36-benchmark comparison with 83.5% accuracy while running 35x faster than GPT-6 Sol. Rival H2O AI has already pushed back on that speed claim, questioning how Microsoft measured competitor latency. Microsoft set pricing at $0.042 per million input tokens with no output charge. Try it here.

Solo developers are using AI to clone big tech software: The developer who cloned seven Adobe apps is back, this time taking on Microsoft Office. Using Claude Opus 5.5, he built and shipped WordCraft, GridCraft, and DeckCraft: open-source Rust clones of Word, Excel, and PowerPoint. Meanwhile, another developer launched DigUp, a Mac app that runs Google's new EmbeddingGemma 2 locally to search files by meaning. While these tools do not match legacy suites yet, they show how far a single developer can get with AI.

AI companies ask Qualcomm for 100B-parameter models on phones: The chipmaker’s CEO, Cristiano Amon, says AI firms want phones running models with at least 100B parameters nonstop by 2028. His bet is that agents tapping your apps and personal context will finally give people a reason to upgrade. The hard part is fitting that model in your pocket. At 4-bit, a 100B model needs about 50GB of RAM for its weights, roughly triple what most flagships carry. For now, distilled models remain the realistic option. Watch the interview.

INSIGHT

A Nobel economist on why AI will replace just 5% of human work this decade

Source: The Code, Superhuman

A smaller number than the hype. If you’ve been bracing for AI to write you out of a job, Nobel economist Daron Acemoglu has a smaller number: about 5% of human tasks over the next decade. Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman just spotlighted the forecast from The Humanist Review, Microsoft AI’s new magazine. Acemoglu’s bigger worry is that replacement fear, shared by 52% of Americans, is steering the industry toward the wrong kind of AI.

The gains haven’t shown up yet. AI’s productivity boom still hasn’t landed in the statistics. Most firms using it see little gain, and engineers can spend plenty of time debugging the code it churns out. Acemoglu expects about 1.5% added to GDP over a decade. Electricity was slow too. New York and London had power stations by 1881, but only about half of factories and homes used it by the 1920s. AI may be slower still, since companies must reorganize around it.

The last 1% does the damage. A system that’s right 99% of the time sounds ready to ship. Acemoglu argues the remaining 1% is where full automation falls apart, as anyone who has chased a production edge case knows. He also says making AI think like a human is the wrong target. The two work differently, so the bigger payoff comes from pairing them.

The case for a sidekick. Instead, he advocates for pro-worker AI: tools that help people do their jobs better, which developers can build today. But incentives reward replacement. The US taxes labor at over 25% and capital at close to zero, effectively subsidizing automation. His most direct appeal is to the people writing the code: if enough developers and engineers made it their priority, he argues, plenty of these tools would follow. You can read the full essay here.

How to reverse engineer any app with Claude Code

An AI builder shared REA (Reverse Engineer Anything), an open-source toolkit that hit #1 on GitHub’s daily trending. It gives Claude Code or Codex the tools to take apart desktop apps, websites, and Android apps, then help you build your own version of a feature.

  • Step 1: Install it and pick your agent when prompted:
npx rea-agents setup
  • Step 2: Restart your agent, then send this prompt:
Use REA to investigate how [app]'s [feature] works. Confirm it's my own software, open source, or allows reverse engineering before starting. Identify the program type, back every finding with evidence (file, function, address), and keep observations separate from guesses. Then propose the minimum pieces I'd need to build my own version — plan first, code after I approve.

Claude works down layer by layer and cites where each finding comes from. Stick to your own apps, open source, or software whose terms permit it.
