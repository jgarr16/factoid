12 free tools for monitoring webpages and keywords

A good investigator is always watching. That means following social media profiles and online communities, monitoring webpages and keywords, tracking assets like ships and airplanes, and searching for new records and documents.

This guide covers 12 free tools for tracking changes to webpages — in some cases entire websites — and for monitoring the web for keywords and search queries. Some are longtime stalwarts like Google Alerts and Visualping; others are lesser-known or open-source options worth adding to your toolkit. One, Scoutpost, is a product we'll offer in the soon-to-launch Indicator Lab package; I explain that connection below.

Here’s a look at how to automate some key monitoring tasks, and a comparison chart to help you pick the right tool.

Keyword monitoring

Created with Claude Design

There aren’t a ton of free options in this category. The most powerful options are expensive media monitoring/social listening platforms. But many people can’t afford such extravagances. I suggest three options, two of which can do general web keyword monitoring (Google Alerts, Talkwalker Alerts), and one that’s mainly useful for tracking Reddit (F5Bot).

For methodology, test and iterate on keywords and search strings. If you already have search strings that you regularly use in search engines, try them out as alerts. Or start with broad keywords and drill down to fine tune your results. Too many investigators set an alert and leave it on autopilot, even when it yields noise or irrelevant results.

I suffer from a different affliction: I keep Google Alerts active long after their utility has passed. For example, I still have an alert for a deceased man I wrote about back in 2018. But I can't bring myself to delete it. What if someone writes about him again! It's the monitoring addict's disease. A healthier approach is regularly adding, editing, and pruning keyword monitors.

One more tip: redundancy is good. I run the same alerts in Google and Talkwalker because they often turn up different results. I recommend running alerts for your most important searches across multiple services.

F5Bot

This is a specialized keyword monitoring tool that pulls results from Reddit, Hacker News, and Lobsters, which is similar to Hacker News but is more focused on computer science and programming. Reddit is the most appealing of the three for investigators.

F5Bot has a generous free plan that provides enough features for basic monitoring. It does not support boolean or advanced operators. You can’t use AND, OR, NOT/- or wildcards in your queries. As it says in its documentation:

“If you enter the keyword fish and chips, F5Bot will only find the complete phrase fish and chips. It will not match fish chips, fishchips, or fish sticks and chips.“ — F5Bot

It also doesn’t give hits on a Reddit/HackerNews/Lobsters username, so that’s not a viable keyword.

Along with tracking keyword(s), the free version can restrict results to one, two, or all of the platforms, and to only look for the keyword in the URL.

I’ve had good success with getting hits for Reddit posts. The free plan gives five alerts with up to 20 results per day.

Google Alerts

The OG of keyword-based web alerts. Google Alerts is one of the best options for general keyword monitoring because Google draws results from its Google search index. That means it has impressive global coverage and can sometimes pull up results from obscure websites.

Anyone with a Google account can set up hundreds of keyword monitors, with results flowing into your inbox (or RSS feed) at a designated schedule. One downside is that it has limited or no results from platforms like Discord and others that aren’t publicly indexable. However, the ability to use boolean and advanced search operators and to set a variety of preferences enables you to be creative and strategic with the alerts you set up.

If you have search queries that you run regularly, transfer them to Google Alerts so they run on an ongoing basis. I strongly encourage you to test out Alert searches that use the “-,” AND, and OR operators to remove noisy results. You can also set up Alerts to focus on results from a specific site (or group of sites) using site:.

One nice feature is that Google Alerts allows you to preview the result after you enter in your query and set preferences. As an example, I created an alert to track incidents where people have been arrested for using an SMS blaster. I entered this search query

_(SMS blaster OR imsi catcher OR "fake cell tower") AND (arrest OR charged OR caught OR sentenced)

I could further customize them by clicking on “Show options”:

That brings up a menu:

I recommend toggling the Sources option to preview what the results look like if you restrict them to a specific type of content, such as News or Web. It’s good to run a quick check. Do the same for “How many”: see how the results change if you change it to “All results.” I often find that it's too noisy, but you should always test it.

I typically start with “At most once a day” for frequency, “Automatic” sources, and “Only the best results.” But it’s important to test the results before you lock it in.

Another option to consider: you can have alerts arrive in an email inbox or export them as an RSS feed. Just change your selection in the “Deliver to” menu. RSS is a great option if you have a lot of alerts and don't want a messy inbox.

Aside from that, remember to prune and edit your alerts to ensure they’re delivering value. Don't be afraid to set multiple for the same project. As long as you delete the ones you no longer need, you can manage the flow. The general consensus seems to be that Google allows around 500 alerts per account so you’re unlikely to hit the limit.

Talkwalker Alerts

The best Google Alerts companion. It’s very similar, right down to the options for an alert and the fact that you can preview results:

Talkwalker supports a variety of boolean operators, but only lets you use a maximum of nine per query. The operators include classic booleans, wildcards, site:, as well as these fun options:

  • Find articles that mention a country. Example: articlecountry:de restricts results to articles that mention Germany.

  • Find articles where the source is from a specific country. Example: sourcecountry:de restricts results to sources from Germany.

  • Deliver results for keywords within proximity to each other. Example: obama merkel~5 would find “every entry, containing the keywords obama and merkel within an interval of maximum of 5 jumps,” according to Talkwalker.

In my experience, Talkwalker Alerts kick up more results from X than Google, though the search giant’s alerts sometimes contain them as well. As previously noted, I often run the same search on Google Alerts and Talkwalker to ensure I cover my bases.

Webpage/site monitoring

Created with Claude Design

Whether you’re waiting for a government website to update with new data or want to track changes across an entire site, you’ve got lots of free options. I found nine website monitoring tools with a free tier, including some that are completely free and open-source.

Core functionality is consistent across tools: tracking entire pages or specific selectors, delivering change alerts, and maintaining version history.

Feature sets vary significantly beyond these core capabilities. The chart below outlines some of the differences and gives a sense of what you can do for free. All of the listed tools have a useful free tier, but some are more generous than others. A good option for journalists is Visualping because it offers free access to track 25 pages with 1,000 checks per month. Just apply here. You can also sign up for a free MuckRock account to get access to the recently relaunched Klaxon, which has fewer features but doesn't limit the number of pages you can monitor.

Just remember that there’s an element of OPSEC to consider with webpage monitoring. If you're using a tool that hits the same page/site from the same IP daily or multiple times a day, it could reveal that someone is tracking the page. Many of the tools let you change the IP or location of your monitoring. It’s something to keep in mind, especially if you check the same page or site frequently and the site owner could be monitoring their logs.

This is especially important if you use an open-source tool or the free Distill alerts that run from your machine. The target website(s) will be able to see your IP. You can obscure this with a VPN, but the IP will still be logged. Whether anyone on the other end notices is tough to predict. You could get blocked or the IP could be used to fingerprint your system and, in the worst case scenario, be combined with other data to identify you.

A disclosure: I included Scoutpost on this list. This tool is built by Tom Vaillant, the developer Indicator partnered with to roll out a new offering of AI-assisted tools for investigators. Scoutpost will be part of that package. It differs from others on the list because it goes beyond webpage tracking to include ships, planes, and social media profiles. But I included it because it has webpage/website monitoring capabilities.

Changedetection.io

This is an open-source webpage monitor with a reasonably priced paid tier. You can install the tool and run it from your machine for free. The paid option allows you to monitor 5,000 URLs, use proxies in Europe, US, or via Tor, and receive change notifications via Discord, email, Slack, Telegram, and webhooks. At $8.99/month it’s a good value among the paid options.

The free and paid versions can connect to an LLM of your choice to help filter and summarize results. The tool can also log into a site (or add a product to a shopping cart) as part of the monitoring process. It offers standard features, such as tracking a specific part of a webpage.

The installation process for Python, Docker, and Windows is pretty straightforward. I got the open source version up and running quickly. (Claude installed it for me with Python.) This is what the interface looked like the first time I accessed it:

The free version has the same features as the paid version if you install via Docker; the difference is that you can use their infrastructure and proxies, which improves OPSEC (as it's not coming from your IP) and enables you to scale up your monitoring. The simpler Python/pip install doesn't include a browser, so you lose anything that needs one: JavaScript rendering, automated screenshots, the point-and-click visual selector, and the Browser Steps feature for logging into a site. You can still target part of a page with a CSS selector or xPath — you just have to type it instead of clicking it.

ChangeTower

Another full featured webpage monitoring tool. One nice feature is that you don’t have to fiddle with making selections on the page to identify what you want to monitor. You can tell it in plain language (“Tell me when the data in the table labeled results changes in any way.”). You can also do a bulk upload.

This is what the Smart Monitor interface looks like. It’s an example of how LLMs are changing the webpage monitoring game. Now you can just say what you want to watch:

Once you create your monitor you can add it to a folder, tag it and choose how frequently you want the page checked. You can also add pages using the free Chrome extension. Note that the ability to monitor a full site is only offered in paid plans. And you can only monitor three pages for free.

Distill

This was the first webpage monitoring tool I used. It seemed like magic back in the day. It has the same core monitoring features as other platforms (full page, parts of page, form filling, PDFs, etc.). I typically monitor pages using the free Chrome extension.

Distill’s free plan allows you to run 5 alerts in the cloud (meaning that Distill does the checking) and 20 alerts that run locally on your machine. You can receive a maximum of 30 email alerts a month for free.

The local monitoring option opens the webpage in your browser to look for changes. That can be annoying and it raises an OPSEC issue because you’re hitting the page(s) from your IP. It can be obscured if you're using a VPN, but it’s worth keeping in mind. Proxy access is available in the paid tier.

On the plus side, Distill’s browser extension works with Chrome, Firefox, Edge, and Opera. It also has iOS and Android apps. You can receive change alerts via Slack, email, Discord, MS Teams, and webhooks.

Klaxon

This began as an in-house site monitoring tool built by the Marshall Project and was released as an open-source project. It’s still open source but a cloud version moved to MuckRock, the journalism tech and research nonprofit, and was integrated into DocumentCloud.

Earlier this month, MuckRock released a revamped version of Klaxon Cloud. It now runs via a Chrome or Firefox extension. Klaxon is completely free if you sign up for a free MuckRock account. A unique feature is that “every time Klaxon Cloud runs, it adds a new capture to the Wayback Machine, then compares against the previous snapshot to find changes.” So your monitoring targets are added to the public archive. (If you’d rather not create a public record, none of the other tools do this.)

Klaxon also draws on the Wayback Machine’s View Changes feature to give you a side by side snapshot of how versions of the page have evolved:

As with other tools, you can set the change detection frequency, track specific parts of a page, and receive alerts by email and Slack. Klaxon’s FAQ acknowledges that it lacks some features found in commercial competitors. “Klaxon Cloud is intentionally designed as a simple and lightweight tool, providing essential website monitoring services for free to anyone with a MuckRock account,” it says.

It doesn't connect to an LLM and it can’t fill out forms or enter logins. The appeal is that it’s totally free, works in browser extensions as opposed to requiring a more technical installation, and saves captures to the Wayback Machine. The page checks run via GitHub Actions, so your IP isn’t exposed.

OnWebChange

Another full featured change tracking platform, though it only offers three pages on the free tier. It says it can track PDFs, JavaScript-heavy pages, and login-protected pages.

Scoutpost

As previously disclosed, Scoutpost is part of Indicator's soon-to-launch offering of tools to help you use AI to assist with OSINT investigations.

Scoutpost uses agents to monitor websites and deliver AI-generated summaries of what changed, while archiving the related info. But it’s a bit different from the other tools because you can also use it to monitor a ship, plane, social media account, local government council, and topics related to your beat or areas of investigation. The free plan offers 100 monthly credits, which you can use to monitor webpages, social accounts, and beats. You can also use MCP, CLI or API to connect it to your preferred AI tool.

Versionista/Fluxguard

Versionista is now part of the Fluxguard webpage monitoring suite. The free tier allows you to monitor three pages. Fluxguard highlights that it uses “an advanced AI crawler designed to monitor websites for even the most subtle changes.” The free tier offers AI translation into English but not change summarization. The paid plans also offer proxies, monitoring pages behind a login, and more.

Visualping

Visualping offers a generous tier for journalists: 1,000 checks across 25 pages. That’s a nice step up from the 150 checks and five pages you get with the standard free tier. Visualping offers more than 30 checking locations, the most diverse location option of any tool I looked at.

It can notify you of changes via email, SMS, MS Teams, Slack, webhooks or API. (Some of those options are only in paid tiers.) Two unique features: you can send alerts to a Google spreadsheet and view changes with a nice side-by-side comparison option (this is an example of the feature from Visualping’s site):

It also has team collaboration functions that I didn’t see advertised elsewhere. Of course, that’s not in the free version.

Wachete

This tool has all the standard page monitoring options but also says it can track Word and Excel files on a site, in addition to PDFs. Wachete’s site says you can use the tool to monitor TLS certificate expirations, JSON/API endpoints, and you can connect it to an AI model via MCP.

One real differentiator is the pricing model. Wachete allows you to check a page as many times as you want in a month. It just limits how many pages you can monitor and how often they’re checked. For example, the free tier lets you check up to five static and one interactive webpage every 24 hours per month. The paid tiers check a page every hour, 10 minutes, every 5 minutes in the highest tier. Most other paid tools I examined typically check every five minutes, but they may cap the total number of page checks per month.

An interesting option related to OPSEC (and to being able to track pages) is that the Wachete FAQ says that you can use your own custom proxy. “This allows you to use datacenter or residential proxies in any location of your selection and limitation is only the offer of the proxy provider,” it says. “You can pick any proxy provider supporting username & password authentication, e.g. Smartproxy or Oxylabs.”
