Today’s top stories

▸ Nvidia’s board held urgent talks to discuss Jensen Huang’s future at the firm. Should he be ousted, there was speculation that Colette Kress, the chipmaker’s chief financial officer, might take over as interim chief executive. Earlier the New York Times reported that Mr Huang, who is being investigated over his relationship with a suspected Chinese spy, had a separate affair with an Nvidia employee. The chipmaker’s share price plunged by almost 9% in after-hours trading.

▸ Shares inNvidia’s rivals surged. Among those to benefit were AMD and Broadcom, two other American chipmakers, as well as Chinese chipmakers such as SMIC, whose stock rose by as much as 5.2%. Other tech stocks also jumped after Coreweave, a “neocloud” that rents out specialist AI chips, reported earnings that beat analysts’ expectations.

▸ Mississippi elected Scott Colom to the Senate. After defeating the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, by a narrow majority, he will be the southern state’s first Democratic senator since the 1980s— and its first black senator since the Reconstruction era. Democrats will control at least 55 of 100 seats in the upper chamber; the race in Alaska is not yet called.

▸ Around 4m workers took part in Spain’sfirst general strike in 14 years, according to labour unions. Some clashes broke out as thousands of people also took to the streets in big cities, demanding measures to tackle a housing crisis. Pedro Sánchez, the prime minister, has called snap elections later this month, following his failure to get his housing reforms through parliament.

▸ Russia andUkraine agreed to their largest prisoner exchange since the war began. However, American-brokered talks produced little progress over steps to end the fighting. American envoys will travel to Moscow on Thursday to meet Vladimir Putin. Volodymyr Zelensky said there was a “window of opportunity” to achieve “a result before 2027”.

▸ Indonesia’s vice-president resigned, putting pressure on Prabowo Subianto, the populist president. The Supreme Court had ruled that Gibran Rakabuming Raka’s education credentials were insufficient to hold office (even though he has a university degree). Mr Gibran is the son of former president Joko Widodo, whose endorsement helped Mr Prabowo win in 2024. Indonesia’s economy has suffered from the president’s misfiring policies; a fracturing of the political elite could make things worse.

▸ United Airlines said it would ban personal AI agents from its platforms, citing the risk of data-privacy violations. Third-party bots, such as Meta’s Muse, will not be able to book their humans tickets or check in for flights. Amazon has also restricted agents from transacting on its site— although the e-emporium’s boss, Andy Jassy, has said he is “hopeful” that could change in future.

Today’s agenda

AP

Europe’s diplomats double down in Dublin

Dublin will host two European summits this week, on Thursday and Friday, offering leaders a chance to discuss the continent’s economic and security vulnerabilities. At the first around 50 leaders will gather for the European Political Community, a loose forum which includes Britain, Ukraine and other non-EU countries. (Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, will join as a middle-power guest.) The gathering is an opportunity for bilateral diplomacy, particularly over Ukraine.

For the second the 27 leaders of EU countries will meet for an informal sit-down. They are meant to discuss how the bloc intends to regulate artificial intelligence (various AI grandees will also be attending). But the urgent situation in Ukraine will probably dominate once again. The EU needs to nominate an official to represent it at peace talks involving America, Russia and Ukraine. Also, as much as €70bn must be found to keep Ukraine in the fight in the coming months.

Britain’s growth spurt could soon end

Figures published on Thursday are expected to show that the British economy grew by 0.7% in the third quarter of 2026. Growth has beaten expectations throughout the year, bolstered by demand from households and spending by businesses on AI. Computing-related services alone grew by 7% in the eight months to August compared with the same period in 2025.

Yet the party might soon be over. Thursday’s data are also expected to show that GDP stopped growing in September, as the costs of the war in the Middle East began to bite. The global energy crisis caused by the conflict will probably continue to weaken the economy for the rest of the year. The Bank of England raised interest rates to 4% on November 5th to contain inflation, and more hikes are expected. Higher energy prices and tighter lending conditions could make Britain’s recent growth spurt little more than a pleasant memory. Unless, that is, further surprises from AI keep the party going.

Cisco tests the data centre boom

Cisco, a maker of networking gear, reports earnings on Thursday. Not long ago the firm was buoyed by the craze for data centres. Its stock surged by 53% in the three months to June as investors bought into its ambition to be a major infrastructure provider for the artificial-intelligence boom. In October, Cisco announced it was expanding its partnership with Nvidia, the world’s largest chipmaker (now rattled by a corporate scandal), to create new kit for such centres, including fancy “pluggables” that can move more data per unit of electricity than previous devices.

If investors had a worry, it was that Cisco would bet too little on AI. Now they may fear the opposite. Markets became queasy about AI infrastructure after American voters backed anti-data-centre candidates across the country in the midterms. That has led to a 12% drop in Cisco’s stock over the past month. The company’s bosses will have to convince markets either that the boom will last, or that they have other promising projects.

DAVID SIMONDS

Meet your matcha-maker

In recent years, the world has gone mad for matcha. Japan’s exports of the powdered green tea reached 12,600 tonnes last year, three times more than a decade earlier. The country is struggling to keep up with global demand; ageing farmers are retiring without successors and farm hands are in short supply.

On Thursday the first International Matcha Congress in Tokyo will bring together farmers, café owners and researchers to discuss the industry’s troubles and how to ensure production keeps pace with the growing number of thirsty customers. Concerns are also growing about a lack of quality standards and the rise of cheap counterfeits. The congress will propose establishing international rules for matcha grading. Japanese producers hope that new criteria will encourage customers to distinguish their premium varieties from cheaper ones. China now produces 70% of the world’s matcha; the high-end market may be Japan’s cup of tea.
