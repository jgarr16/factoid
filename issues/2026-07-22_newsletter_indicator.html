How tabloid claims about Latvia’s “man drought” fueled a misleading social media frenzy and polluted Google results

“Welcome to Latvia,” says the AI voiceover of a video seen almost 9 million times on TikTok. In this “real life women’s kingdom that single guys dream about,” the video continues, there are “tall, blonde, fair-skinned blue-eyed girls everywhere.”

It’s one of 89 viral posts about Latvia’s alleged “man drought” that I collected across Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. The videos and images refer to the Baltic nation as a “leftover ladies’ paradise” where men “are more valuable than gold” and women are “forced to lower the standards” to find a companion.

“Here, even an average man can be pursued by several women at the same time,” says another video seen 12 million times.

This misleading narrative contains a kernel of truth. In 2025, Latvian women and girls outnumbered their male counterparts by more than 15 percent. But as the chart below shows, the imbalance is entirely concentrated among older people, particularly those above the age of 60. The videos and images, however, feature women who appear to be in their twenties and thirties.

The trope of Latvia as a paradise for passport bros has escaped containment, aided by an unholy alliance of misleading tabloid stories and clickbaity social media posts. It has coincided with spikes in related search queries and polluted Google results about the topic. Researchers have found that UGC websites are the soft underbelly of AI research agents, letting fishy claims slip through on topics where authoritative sources don’t have much to say. Latvia’s “man drought” is one such case study.

It’s not just an information quality issue. Earlier this year, Beata Jonite of the Latvian women’s rights organization MARTA Centre told Deutsche Welle that the man drought narrative “is objectifying” and “makes it seem like for men who wouldn’t be able to get a woman [in] other places, they could come here.”

This narrative is a zombie myth, one that keeps returning regardless of its accuracy.

One of the biggest waves of “man drought" videos in my sample came after a December story in The Sun (later picked up by The New York Post) about Latvian women hiring “husbands by the hour.”

The articles were actually about a handyman service with a catchy marketing tagline.

But that isn’t the only deceptive element. The tabloid stories prominently feature “a glam single lady, Dania, 29” and her friend Zane complaining about Latvia’s gender imbalance. “You would want to have some more men to flirt or chat with,” Dania is quoted as saying. Those exact words were attributed to a 29-year-old Dania in a BBC article published 16 years ago. The Sun and Post articles don’t link to the BBC story or flag the quote as old.

In addition, several of the most viral Instagram posts in my sample showcase a photo of two young women with their sensationalist headlines. Best I could tell, the women aren't even in Latvia.

Through a reverse image search I found the women in a video about the Polish city of Łódź. To double check that the video was actually from Poland, I took the visible Primark logo in the background and searched for the store’s location in Łódź on Google Street View.

The match appears conclusive.

All this clickbait has actually driven users to search for information about Latvia’s gender imbalance. In India and the United States, searches like “Latvia men shortage” and “Latvia gender ratio” were what Google calls “breakout queries” in 2025. Both grew more than 5,000% compared to the year before.

They also appear to have coincided with spikes in search queries for Latvia overall. Correlation does not prove causation, but especially in India the co-occurring spikes in queries about the Baltic country and those about its “man drought” are suggestive (see chart below).

To see how well the search engine dealt with this influx of interest, I scraped the results for 75 queries about Latvia’s demographic imbalance across three different styles of queries: “leading,” “neutral,” and “fact-checking.”

The leading bucket includes queries like “Latvia paradise for men” and “Latvia women can’t find men” that directly evoke language used in the viral videos. This is where Google might be expected to do worse as it balances relevance versus authoritativeness.

Indeed, several AI Overview responses accepted or repeated the misleading framing. A few said there is a “noticeable imbalance among younger adults” or that it “also affects dating-age populations.” Both are untrue. Most of the AI-generated responses linked out to at least one of the clickbaity videos in my sample.

Neutral queries like “Latvia census gender” or “Latvia demographic report” performed better. AI Overviews presented a balanced response and linked back to the misleading videos in only seven of the 25 cases.

The “fact-checking” group of queries elicited a more interesting mix of responses. Many did a good job at dispelling the myth that a young man might find innumerable potential partners in Latvia. However, 19 out of 25 responses prominently linked to clickbaity videos that spread false information.

Across the board, Google Search results were affected by the plethora of misleading videos about Latvia’s alleged “man drought.”

While 64 of 75 Google’s AI Overviews tested did provide some context about the age skew in the demographic imbalance, many did so only after the full response was expanded. Moreover, a majority of responses (39/75) directly linked to clickbait content.
