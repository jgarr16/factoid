How TikTok bots turned a cancer patient's story into engagement bait

More than 150 inauthentic accounts posted content stolen from Sydney Towle, a cancer patient with more than one million followers who passed away Aug. 5.

EJ Gibney

Sydney Towle was 23 when doctors diagnosed her with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, a rare and aggressive bile duct cancer.

Towle documented her illness and treatment on TikTok to more than 1 million followers. Her videos regularly drew tens of thousands of likes and thousands of comments filled with well-wishes and prayers for her recovery.

Not everyone responded positively. A May 2025 New York Times article detailed how Towle received online hate and even death threats from people who wrongly believed she was faking her cancer for profit.

But over the past six months, a second, quieter form of exploitation has formed around her videos. Indicator identified a network of 152 deceptive TikTok accounts that repost Towle’s content with slight alterations in an effort to farm engagement.

The accounts, which exhibited signs of automation, use sympathy to siphon audience and engagement away from Towle while misleading users. Many accounts in the network have more than the 10,000 followers required to potentially earn money from TikTok’s creator monetization program. Videos posted by the accounts often receive tens or hundreds of thousands of views.

Melanie Bosselait, a TikTok spokesperson, said the accounts identified by Indicator would be removed and confirmed that they were automated.

Bosselait said in an emailed statement.

The company declined to say whether any of the accounts were monetized.

Indicator sent a request for comment to Towle and her team on July 15. She died on August 5, one day after entering hospice care. Indicator did not receive a response before her passing.

Eight automated accounts that stole Towle’s content.

Engagement bait

The accounts shared similarities that suggest they’re part of the same operation and were automated: their usernames were random letters and numbers, they often used profile photos that are cartoons or anime, more than half of the accounts didn’t follow anyone, the videos were posted within seconds or minutes of each other, and the accounts added the same captions and backgrounds to Towle’s videos.

A typical stolen Towle video replaces the background with the interior of a house, a landscape, or a hospital room, adding on-screen text such as “How can two doctors see the complete opposites on scans?!?!? 5/28/2026,” “2026six2 MRI that shows atrophicvaries doesn’t mean it’s notpossible [sic] to conceive,” and “You poor thing. My heart breaks for you. I hate cancer.”

Twelve duplicate videos of Sydney Towle, slightly altered by the user with additional text.

The caption "@syd, I'm so sorry this happened to you" appears in some form on more than 1,100 videos. Over 700 of the videos displayed the same caption error: it cut off mid-word at "@TheNe," a broken reference to The New York Times. The error, along with other indicators, suggested that the network was automated.

Many videos contained captions that appeared to be AI-generated or SEO-driven strings of text geared towards influencing search results. Examples include, “The doctor is discussing palliative care. Despite the diagnosis, this 26-year-old woman stated that she refused to give up” and “Syd Faty Syd Kell Sydwing Call Me Syd Sydsweet Syd Syd Sloth Syd Squid Syd Lol Syd Mami Sydney Towle's Journey with Chemo and Surgery Join Sydney Towle as she navigates her cancer journey.”

In another sign of automation, accounts posted within a tight window. More than a third of all videos in the sample were posted within five minutes of each account's previous video. Many were posted within seconds of each other.

A review of comments indicated that viewers often believed the accounts belong to Towle. People share messages like “Praying for your recovery” and “Just one of your MANY [sic] followers who is a complete stranger invested in your journey.”

A collection of user comments left in response to inauthentic duplicate Sydney Towle videos.

Chinese-language artifacts

A collection of four inauthentic videos with on-screen text in Chinese. Translated, the text is shortcode for the heart emoji.

It’s unclear who operates the accounts or where they're located. There are, however, indicators that at least some of the accounts were created or maintained by a person or people who speak Chinese and/or are based in China. Some videos included "原創音樂" as an audio credit. It means "original sound," and was there in place of the typical label TikTok displays on English-speaking accounts. Other accounts featured on-screen captions with "[爱心][爱心][爱心]," a Chinese-language shortcode for the heart emoji that failed to render as a visual emoji before the video was posted. Such artifacts could indicate a Chinese-language app, service, or AI tool somewhere in the content-generation pipeline.

A new type of attack

Towle spent more than a year dealing with often-aggressive strangers convinced she was lying about her illness. This time, at least, no one accused her of lying. Instead, they profited from the fact that she wasn’t.

Indicator Info Box

  • I found the first handful of inauthentic accounts through manual TikTok searches for terms tied to Sydney Towle's name and diagnosis. From there, I used the shared captions, cross-referenced hashtags, and TikTok’s algorithm to find additional accounts.

  • The 19-digit number at the end of every TikTok URL is built so that the video's exact creation time is embedded inside it. I used a spreadsheet formula to extract the timestamp for every video in the network.

  • Once every video had a creation time attached, a second spreadsheet formula measured the gap in seconds between each video and the one before it across the whole network, and within each account.

  • I manually logged each account's follower count, following list, likes, and total video count at the time of collection.

  • Google Lens and Google Translate were used to translate the Chinese text to English.
