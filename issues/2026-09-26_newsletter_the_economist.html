Today’s top stories

▸ OpenAI said that its artificial-intelligence agents may have hacked “dozens” of websites of international institutions, including governments, universities and public agencies. Earlier this week it was revealed that the firm’s rogue AI agents had accessed data from an Australian health agency. Amidst a slew of such incidents, Sam Altman, OpenAI’s boss, has called for the pace of artificial-intelligence development to be slowed.

▸ The Wall Street Journal reported that Donald Trump had rejected a seven-day ceasefire deal offered by Iran on Thursday. The proposal could have led to the re-opening of the Strait of Hormuz, however the Journal says that Mr Trump is skeptical of Iran’s sincerity. The president has said that he expects the war to continue until after the midterms in November.

▸ America’s Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to continue using a broad database that lets states verify the citizenship of registered voters. A federal judge previously blocked the programme’s deployment. Mr Trump says the system, which his administration expanded to include immigration and social-security records, will stop non-citizens from voting; critics argue that it has already removed eligible voters from state rolls by mistake.

▸ ArcelorMittal said it was stopping production at Ukraine’sbiggest steel plant. Europe’s largest steelmaker said that it was unable to conduct “safe and sustainable” operations after Russia attacked the plant four times in five weeks, killing five workers. Steel once made up 15% of Ukraine’s exports. Russia has been pummeling the country’s infrastructure with ballistic missiles and, recently, jet-powered drones to cripple its economy.

▸ Manchester City was reportedly found guilty of all but one of 115 charges of breaching the English Premier League’s financial rules. The football club is accused of misrepresenting its finances, breaking spending rules and failing to co-operate with investigators. Punishments could range from fines and points deductions to expulsion from the league, the world’s most lucrative. City has denied wrongdoing and is expected to appeal.

▸ Brightline, a high-speed railway service based in Florida, filed for bankruptcy. The private equity-backed firm is seeking to cut nearly $6bn in debt after years of disappointing passenger numbers. Brightline was seen as a test to whether private companies could run intercity railway lines in America, where public-transit systems are sparse. It operated the first privately owned rail service in the country, linking Miami and Orlando.

▸ Heidenau, a town in Saxony, eastGermany, voted to stop laying down memorial Stolpersteine— brass paving stones that commemorate victims of the Holocaust. Since 1992 such “stumbling stones” have been installed outside the houses of known victims across Europe. The motion was introduced by the Christian Democratic Union and the far-right Alternative for Germany party, whose co-founder once referred to Germany’s Nazi past as “bird poop”.

Pressure builds on India’s election body

Two months ago, the Cockroach Janta Party took to the streets across India to protest against exam-paper leaks, forcing the resignation of the country’s education minister. Now the youth-led movement wants another scalp: Gyanesh Kumar. Should the head of India’s election body not resign on Saturday, the CJP has threatened another round of nationwide protests.

The demand follows an explosive report this week by the Indian Express. The newspaper alleged that Mr Kumar had overridden objections from his fellow election commissioners about how voters were struck off electoral rolls. The objections emerged during an ongoing nationwide exercise to weed out ineligible voters. Since the process started, opposition parties have claimed that legitimate voters have been struck off, which has helped India’s ruling Bharatiya Janata Party in recent state polls. Both the BJP and the election commission deny the charges. But that has done little to quell growing anger. The CJP’s first protest rattled the government; the second one could shake India’s democracy.

Turkey’s market meltdown

The numbers seemed too good to be true. Dozens of investment funds— and the financial groups behind them— posted spectacular returns, propelling Turkey’s stock market to record highs. Then the bubble burst. Several asset managers failed to pay investors seeking to cash out. Details emerged of alleged Ponzi-like schemes. Last week, the scandal wiped some $30bn from the country’s market in just two days.

Share prices have since steadied, and the crisis has not spread to bond or currency markets. But the fallout continues. Scores of finance executives have been detained, accused of market manipulation. More arrests may follow as investigators probe suspicious bank transfers. To mop up the mess, Turkey’s market regulator has moved to liquidate 131 investment funds. They are estimated to hold roughly $18bn in assets between them. At least 450,000 investors are now bracing for a painful haircut and a long wait for whatever money remains.

A new era for triathlon

This weekend the World Triathlon will crown its champions in Pontevedra, Spain. The competition is one of four elite tours— the others are the Professional Triathletes Organisation, Ironman and Supertri. While that may provide more opportunities for athletes, it has fragmented audiences, holding the sport back from becoming as popular as it could be. But change is afoot (having first been afloat and then a-pedal). Next year World Triathlon and PTO will merge their championships, becoming the Triathlon World Tour.

Triathlon has the potential to become a big business. Its combination of swimming, cycling and running means it can appeal to a wider range of fans. Organisers of the new event say it could create momentum ahead of the 2028 Olympic Games in Los Angeles. (Triathlon made its debut at the Olympics in 2000.) They also plan to boost amateur races, potentially growing crowds— and finding the star triathletes of the future.

A rural festival puts roadkill on the menu

Turtle stew, anyone? That is one dish that might be sampled at Saturday’s Roadkill Cookoff & Autumn Harvest Festival in Pocahontas, a large and sparsely populated county in West Virginia. The animals being cooked do not have to be scraped off the tarmac. They just need to be the sort that could feasibly be run over. Instead of chicken, beef or pork, think bear, groundhog and snake.

For culinary adventurers, the draw is the chance to eat exotic meats that have been skilfully prepared. The majority of West Virginia’s population is rural, and it is the only state located entirely within the Appalachian mountains— a region known for culinary traditions that emphasise resourcefulness, such as hunting, pickling and drying. Even the most ardent vegans might still find a way to enjoy themselves: few places in America are more picturesque this time of year than West Virginia. Just make sure to look up at the golden leaves instead of towards the kitchen.

Weekend profile: Christoph Blocher, the controversial billionaire fighting for Swiss neutrality

Christoph Blocher tries hard to be the friendly face of Swiss nationalism. A jokey campaign video shows the billionaire, who is the most prominent member of the country’s dominant, populist-right Swiss People’s Party (SVP), belly-flopping into a pool and cutting the grass with scissors at his mansion on Lake Zurich. He has appeared alongside a goat, the party’s mascot, leading a parade of people in traditional alpine outfits. But such joviality belies Mr Blocher’s sharp-edged politics. Steve Bannon, a former adviser to America’s president, has called him “Trump before Trump”.

Now the 85-year-old is waging perhaps his final political battle. He is the driving force behind a referendum on Sunday that would see Switzerland adopt a stricter definition of its centuries-old policy of official neutrality, barring it from peacetime co-operation with NATO and from imposing sanctions on other countries. Though it will probably be defeated, the plebiscite, which is openly supported by Russia, has ignited debate about how neutral a European country should be, given Russian aggression and an increasingly unreliable America. Mr Blocher sees strict adherence as the only way to protect Switzerland from war. His opponents argue that Switzerland must at least be able to co-operate with European sanctions, which would be barred if it passes.

Mr Blocher was born to a Protestant pastor and his wife in 1940, the seventh of 11 children. He had a modest upbringing in the canton of Zurich and initially planned to become a farmer. Instead he studied law before joining EMS-Chemie, a then-struggling chemicals company. In 1983 he acquired a majority stake for 16m Swiss francs ($7.6m), funded in part by selling his collection of art by Albert Anker, an 19th-century painter of patriotic Swiss scenes. Once he had turned the firm into a successful multinational corporation, “What mattered to me first was recovering the works I had to sell,” he told a reporter in 2012. Today he keeps them as part of a sprawling art collection in a private museum below his mansion.

After he joined the SVP in 1972 Mr Blocher orchestrated its transformation from a small, agrarian party into an anti-immigration force that is today the largest party in the country’s Federal Assembly. He has warned of “creeping EU membership”— he considers his campaign to keep Switzerland out of the European Economic Area in 1992 his crowning achievement. His opponents have accused him and his party of racism: in 2007 the SVP put out a poster featuring white sheep kicking a black one off a Swiss flag. He lost his seat in an election that same year; since then he has largely campaigned from the sidelines.

Like Mr Trump, he sees no paradox in being a free-market advocate who wants to restrict foreign labour, or a billionaire who rails against the establishment. “It’s not the people but the elites who criticise me for being rich,” he has said. Mr Blocher may have few political fights left, but his type of combative populism is becoming more common across Europe.
