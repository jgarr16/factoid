Meta says Community Notes is bigger than fact-checking. That's not the whole story

Meta said yesterday that it will expand Community Notes, its crowdsourced fact-checking program, to sixteen Latin American countries. As part of the announcement, the company touted the fact that in the US pilot “the daily volume of Community Notes has doubled independent fact-checks.”

It may be true that more corrective information is being added to posts in the US through Community Notes than through the fact-checking partnership Meta has wound down. But the target of such corrections is very different from before.

An Indicator analysis found that just 3.0% of Instagram posts fact-checked by AFP’s US bureau since April 2025 have a corrective note. This is much lower than the 20.5% rate for X’s Community Notes program.

Instagram posts that have gone without a note despite AFP publishing a fact check about them include false claims about children’s vaccines, the health benefits of nicotine pouches, and voter fraud in the NYC mayoral election.1

The low prevalence of Community Notes on debunked Instagram posts suggests – at a minimum – that Meta users aren’t focusing on providing context to the same claims that professional fact-checkers cover. While their overall coverage may be broader, they are not a replacement for the fact-checking partnership axed by Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg as a sop to the Trump administration.

A Meta spokesperson told Indicator that it labels twice as many posts with Community Notes on a given day as under the now defunct US fact-checking program. They also said that Meta’s program is relatively new compared to X’s Community Notes and will need time to establish itself.

Angie Holan, director of the International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN), told Indicator that she worries the program “can't actually prioritize accurate information — that it has the outward appearance of correcting falsehoods while the claims that matter most, especially the polarizing ones, go unnoted.”

Meta has yet to fulfil its promise to the Oversight Board to publish comprehensive data about Community Notes similar to what X discloses. In the absence of greater transparency, some creative auditing is needed to assess their reach.

The program launched in March 2025 and, up to this week, was active only in the United States. To discern its effectiveness, I collected all 836 Instagram and X posts fact-checked by the US branch of AFP Fact Check since April 2025.

I found only five visible notes on the 166 Instagram posts that were fact-checked by AFP and still live on the platform. That’s 3.0% of the total. By contrast, I found 117 visible notes on 572 flagged X posts, which comes out to about 20.5%.

A post on X fact-checked by AFP is almost seven times more likely to carry a Community Note than its equivalents on Instagram, according to my data.

In more than 100 cases, AFP fact checks debunked the same false claim circulating on both Instagram and X. Forty-six drew a note on X but not on Instagram. Three were noted on both. One was noted only on Meta’s platform.

In at least four cases, the posts weren’t just pushing the same false narrative but carried the same exact content by the same author. For example, right-wing YouTuber Nick Shirley’s false claim that 30 people were registered to vote at a UPS store (which shares an address with a residential complex) got 6 million views on X and 8 million views on Instagram. But only the X post had a Community Note when I checked.

On the other side of the US political spectrum, a misleadingly spliced video by congressional candidate Brad Lander got a Community Note on X but not on Lander’s Instagram account.

Both these posts on Instagram had proposed notes on them when I logged on from my US-based account that is signed up to Community Notes. That means someone wrote a fact check but it hasn’t been upvoted by enough other Meta users with a diverse prior voting record to get affixed to the post. (Our sample also included 147 notes that were written but not displayed on X.)

AFP Fact Check told me its journalists check if a social media post already has a Community Note, and use that as a potential signal that a claim is disputed and potentially worth fact-checking. If AFP fact-checkers find significantly more leads through X’s Community Notes than Instagram’s, this could explain the divergence between the two platforms.

I shared my data with Mohsen Mosleh, an associate professor in Social Data Science at Oxford who has extensively researched Community Notes. He told me that Indicator’s audit has a “reasonable and defensible design” because the posts reviewed are “precisely the posts a functioning crowd-notes system should catch.”

But we can’t truly characterize activity on Community Notes with the little data that Meta has shared to date. The company has sporadically shared aggregate statistics, most recently that Community Notes has 1.4 million contributors that have published 50,000 notes. It also provides a searchable portal that a company spokesperson told me provides access to all helpful notes when logged in with a Meta account. After several efforts, I still could only see only a few dozen recent notes.

"Your research strongly suggests community notes aren't appearing on Instagram at anything like the frequency we see on X,” the IFCN’s Angie Holan told Indicator.

“Why not? Could it be that Meta's technical capacity isn't as robust? Could it be that Instagram users are less interested in writing and rating notes? Could it be that contributors on the two platforms are surfacing different kinds of content in the first place? We don't know — and we shouldn't have to guess. Meta promised more data on this program and hasn't delivered it.”

Indicator Info Box

All underlying data is available to Indicator members at this link. Here are few more details about my methodology:

  • I extracted links to Meta platforms and X using Claude’s Chrome Integration, then used Claude Code to isolate links that included text identifying the social post as the focus of the fact check. (This last step was meant to avoid counting social media posts that were used as evidence or context.)

  • Claude’s Chrome integration helped me collect all of the available information for each relevant social post, including the presence of a Community Note.

  • I had inconsistent results with Facebook and Threads, but I was able to reliably detect Community Notes on Instagram (even from Italy) with a US-based Instagram account. So I focused my analysis on that platform.

  • X was easier to review because I could match tweet IDs to publicly-viewable notes from X’s public dataset. Finally, I manually reviewed 10% of all posts to ensure accuracy.
