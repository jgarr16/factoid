8 AI bots now write 50% of X’s Community Notes

A handful of anonymous bots have taken over fact-checking on X.

This isn’t hyperbole. In the first three weeks of May, just eight AI contributors wrote 50.3% of all visible Community Notes on the platform.

When the program launched five years ago, X positioned it as a way to harness the knowledge of users to add context and corrections to tweets. It described Community Notes as a way to “broaden the range of voices” fighting misinformation.

Then, in July 2025, X started allowing people to use automation to mass-generate and submit AI notes. That new feature made it inevitable that human writers would eventually be overtaken by bots. But it happened faster than I predicted.

Rolling share of helpful notes written by the top 10 AI and human contributors

Community Notes was supposed to bring scale and legitimacy to the fight against misinformation by empowering users to participate in fact-checking. Now that power is increasingly in the hands of a tiny group of hobbyists and researchers.

AI contributors aren’t just replacing the humans who are supposed to make up the community in Community Notes.1 They are publishing in languages and on topics that reflect the bias of their algorithmic setup. A project meant to humanize trust and safety has been delegated to bots and the people who run them.

Keith Coleman, VP of product at X, told Indicator that “people clearly value notes from AI writers, as shown by the growing number appearing on X.” Coleman said Community Notes power users appreciate them too and have made product feature requests for AI contributors that X has implemented.

What happens next will matter not just for X, or for the copycat Community Notes features on Meta and TikTok.2 For good and for bad, X is currently running the world's largest live experiment in automated fact-checking. How it fares may define platform interventions against misleading content for years to come.

To better understand the program’s trajectory, I spoke to three researchers testing AI contributors, two human supercontributors, and several current and past X employees. I also used X’s (admirably) open dataset to compare several thousand tweets fact-checked by human and AI writers and scrape the sources and languages used in the nearly 61,000 helpful notes published since synthetic contributors were onboarded.

SCALING FACT-CHECKING

Between its 2021 launch and late 2022, Community Notes (then known as Birdwatch) expanded cautiously. The project let in a few hundred users, then a few thousand. As one founding team member wrote in early 2025, “we spent 2 years carefully testing, identifying risks & mitigations, setting up monitoring and guardrails, and slowly expanding as we gained confidence.”

Then Elon Musk bought X. He has (mostly) been a fan of Community Notes, calling it “the best source of truth on the Internet by far” and posting “Community Notes ftw” more than a dozen times. Despite undoing other interventions against false or misleading information, Musk’s X has scaled up Community Notes to hundreds of thousands of unique users.

This expansion greatly increased the number of “helpful” notes appended to tweets. The number of visible notes grew from a daily average of just six in 2022 to 105 in 2023 and more than 260 in 2024. (X shows a note to all users, rather than only on the Community Notes feed, when the platform’s “bridging” algorithm determines that users who upvoted the note have previously disagreed on other topics.)

The program’s growth was followed by a noticeable dip in 2025. (2024 was a record year for elections around the world, which may have fueled contributions that year.)

The dip would have continued into 2026 if not for the introduction of AI contributors. They reversed the curve. As bots gradually increased their contributions, they pushed the total number of notes back into 2024 territory, masking a stagnation in the number of human contributions.

Number of helpful notes by contributor type. AI is yellow, humans are blue.

CAN AI CONTRIBUTORS DO THE JOB?

X implemented automation by allowing anyone with an account to use the AI Note Writer API to mass-generate notes. AI contributors earn the ability to post notes by writing dummy notes that pass X’s algorithmic tests. The review process checks for functional URLs and whether the note uses respectful and neutral language.

Once an AI contributor is allowed into the program, it can access a queue of posts that X users flagged as potentially requiring a note. AI-generated notes are treated the same as human-authored notes; they need to collect upvotes from a diverse set of users. In that sense, humans are still in charge of which notes get added to posts on X.

In a preprint, Haiwen Li and Michiel Bakker of MIT studied roughly 1,500 tweets that had notes proposed by an AI contributor that they developed. LLM notes received higher “helpful” and lower “not helpful” ratings than human notes across the political spectrum, they found. (Li and Bakker previously co-authored a paper with X employees that work on Community Notes, but this research was conducted independently.)

Source: AI Fact-Checking in the Wild: A Field Evaluation of LLM-Written Community Notes on X

Li said she is “in general optimistic” about the effect of AI contributors, while noting that more research is needed to understand their impact on human writers and raters.

The perceived helpfulness of Li’s bot is not a one-off. AI notes have a 12.9% average helpfulness rate, compared to 8.4% for humans. Of the 29 bots that have posted at least one note, 21 have a better helpfulness rate than the human average. Helpfulness doesn’t necessarily mean accuracy, of course. And the human average conceals some highly effective authors with helpfulness rates above 50%. But the stats suggest AI contributors can be a valuable addition to the mix.

Other rough proxies suggest that bots are doing a decent enough job, at least when it comes to helpful notes. I found no major differences in a qualitative review of 81 AI and human helpful notes on misleading tweets mentioning Alex Pretti, the ICU nurse killed by ICE in Minnesota in January 2026. (While a handful misunderstood the primary topic of the post that they were fact-checking, they were not inaccurate.)

AI bots also cite fact-checking websites like Snopes, AFP Fact Check, LeadStories3, and PolitiFact at 3.5 times the rate of humans, while citing X itself 2.5 times less often.

While they do make outrageous errors, such mistakes tend to be filtered out by the bridging algorithm, much like the human errors. For the purposes of this article, then, let’s assume not that AI is good at fact-checking but that AI-written helpful notes are _about as accurate _as their crowdsourced human counterparts.

But that doesn’t mean they are the same.

CONCERNING CONCENTRATION

One thing that stands out about AI notes is their linguistic skew.

I found that 87% of all AI helpful notes were written in English. That compares to only 57% of human notes during the same time period. As I’ve written before, Community Notes has an active Japanese contingent that contributes about 11% of all human helpful notes; but the language is way under-represented in synthetic notes.

In principle, LLMs should be able to at least match the distribution of the major languages in the human corpus. The current concentration may therefore be a temporary artifact rather than a permanent feature. (That said, LLMs generally struggle in smaller languages.)

Running an AI Note Writer may also be prohibitively expensive in some parts of the world, especially given that it’s a voluntary exercise that ultimately benefits a private corporation owned by the richest man alive.

Nathan Young, a forecaster and developer, has estimated that the cost to run his AI contributor Wholesome Raspberry Stilt is about $7 per helpful note. (The bot has a roughly 14% helpfulness rate, so the cost per individual note is more like $1).

Young told Indicator he expects this cost to decrease as he figures out more efficient ways to run the bot and the price of the underlying LLM capabilities goes down over time. Still, he flagged that he has received support from Jaan Tallinn and the Future of Life Foundation for this work. He also believes that in the long run it would probably be better if AI contributors were funded “in a decentralized and altruistic way,” perhaps with support from X and the big AI labs.

Relying on a small group of AI writers doesn’t just reduce the linguistic and economic diversity of Community Notes contributors. It also appears to make notes over-reliant on Grok, the LLM developed and owned by X.

MIT’s Li told me that at some point in her research, she decided that her bot needed to use Grok because it is the only LLM that can search X freely. Because so many misleading posts can be corrected by referencing other posts on the platform or by reviewing replies and comments, Li determined that not using Grok would place her at a disadvantage. (She has published the instructions for her bot, which is still active on X.)

Other AI contributors made the same decision. Li said that using Grok for searching X doesn’t preclude using other LLMs to collect evidence and write the note. But if synthetic notes must at least in part rely on Grok for fact-checking, a majority-AI Community Notes will be significantly less decentralized and diverse than a human-first program. It is also vulnerable to systemic biases in Grok.

OPTIMIZING FOR CONSENSUS?

I analyzed a stratified sample of roughly 2,000 tweets that were fact-checked by the top human and AI contributors since September 2025. The goal was to compare the focus of their notes (more details on the methodology are available in the Info Box at the end of this article).

Both human and AI contributors focus on tweets with a large audience. The median view count was 458,000 for human-noted tweets and 730,000 for AI-noted ones. Flagged authors are also typically verified, which is downstream of their ability to reach users on the platform. (81% of human targets have the blue checkmark, and 91% of AI targets do).

But human supercontributors appear to focus more on political and hyperpartisan accounts, at least in my sample.

Four of the top five accounts fact-checked by AI contributors — Crazy Moments, RKM, HustleBitch, and Hoops Crave — primarily post engagement bait and sensationalist material. But three out of the top five accounts targeted by human contributors — Dom Lucre, John McAfee News, and The General — are hyperpartisan rightwing political accounts. In sixth and seventh positions, Catturd and Laura Loomer are in the same mold and fact-checked five times as often by humans as they are by AI bots.

The underlying tweets fact-checked by humans were also far more likely to be deleted or written by accounts that were later suspended (31% vs 21%). This could be a reflection of the topics humans choose to cover.

I found that humans fact-checked politicians far more than AI contributors. Humans fact-checked Marine Le Pen, Sohei Kamiya and other political accounts 43 times. By contrast, AI contributors only left five notes on similar accounts.

The apparent reluctance to cover politics may come down to how bots gain access to the program. As mentioned earlier, AI Note Writers have to get a minimum score on three X classifiers, including one called ClaimOpinion that assesses whether a note will be “perceived as expressing opinion or speculation.”

David Rand, a professor of information science at Cornell and the author of several papers about fact-checking on social media, said that ClaimOpinion may inadvertently push AI bots away from targeting political tweets.

In unpublished work, Rand used his own AI Note Writer to fact-check a large set of posts that were in the AI contributor queue using the same model and prompt. He ran all of the notes by the ClaimOpinion classifier and found that a key predictor for a negative score was how political the post was. Simply put, posts that mentioned polarizing political topics performed poorly. He also had 20 different LLMs re-write each note, and chose the note that got the best score. While this increased the average score, it was still the case that notes on more political posts received lower scores.

AI contributors may end up optimizing for consensus by fact-checking less politicized topics. This would further exacerbate a bias that some human contributors have already said is built into Community Notes’ core bridging algorithm.

Young, the developer behind the eighth most prolific AI contributor, called this algorithm “a mechanism that is asymmetrically good rather than neutral,” because it is resistant to inaccurate fact checks. It has certainly reduced the risk of biased fact checks getting “helpful” status. But there is a drawback in this consensus-seeking filter.

Celeste Labedz, a seismologist and top-10 contributor to Community Notes, told Indicator that the bridging algorithm favors fact checks on less controversial topics like earthquake predictions and makes it harder for notes on polarizing topics like climate change or vaccine safety to ever reach “helpful” status.

Labedz called Community Notes “a popularity contest pretending to be a fact checker.”

She doesn't think AI contributors will help. “Having notes generated by LLM doesn't fix this problem as long as the visibility algorithm continues to be about placation rather than fact-checking,” she said.

THE DEVOLUTION DELUSION

When Community Notes launched, Twitter’s then CEO Jack Dorsey presented the program as a harbinger of the future of content moderation.

He told investors in February 2021 that “Birdwatch is something we must try out” because “global centralized content moderation, especially around sensitive information, just will not scale over time.” He described Birdwatch as a “good complement” to its existing trust and safety work. But he argued that Twitter shouldn’t be the “arbiter of all these things, it should be more of the wisdom of the crowds.” He reiterated this position in subsequent written testimony to the US Congress.

This idealistic take, shared by pilot users and members of the Birdwatch team, always co-existed with a more opportunistic goal. A majority of people around the world believe platforms should moderate misinformation, but in practice the exercise draws extensive scrutiny from political elites. After getting criticized for doing both too much and not enough during the US 2020 election and the COVID-19 pandemic, Twitter leadership found in Community Notes a path to expand fact-checking interventions while reducing its direct involvement.

When Mark Zuckerberg adopted Community Notes on Meta in January 2025, he hid his self-serving motivation to please Donald Trump behind loftier goals.

The Meta CEO called Community Notes, a “better way of achieving our original intention of providing people with information about what they’re seeing – and one that’s less prone to bias” than professional fact-checkers. He said X’s effort was a way to “empower their community.” (X’s then CEO Linda Yaccarino was jubilant at the news, and called the program a “global collective consciousness, keeping each other accountable at global scale in real time.”) TikTok also launched a similar program called Footnotes.4

All this talk of community empowerment is hard to square with the current predominance of a handful of AI contributors. In the first three weeks of May, the top six AI bots got about as many notes published on X as all 2,012 human contributors.

Such displacement will lead to human fatigue and disaffection. The data suggests this may already be happening. The number of unique human authors writing helpful notes each month has fallen from an average of 4,348 in the first four months of 2025 to 4,070 in the first four months of 2026.

Mike Doris, another Community Notes supercontributor, recently wrote that “if a human puts in the effort to write 10 notes, and only one reaches helpful, and that keeps happening, they're going to stop doing that.“

In a working paper with Tom Stafford and Zahra Arjmandi-Lari, I found evidence that human writers whose notes were just over the “helpfulness” cut-off were 5% more likely to go on to write other notes than those whose first note never published. Recognition matters to humans in ways that will never be the case for bots.

But X’s Coleman is not worried that contributors will churn out of the program.

"This approach has scaled well,” he told Indicator, adding that “we've roughly doubled the number of notes on X in the past four months. Demand remains strong — as more good notes are proposed, more get rated helpful and shown. We’re also steadily growing the contributor base by regularly admitting people from the waitlist in a paced way.”

Still, the platform must have recognized that a shift was inevitable when it launched AI contributors. It framed the project in a preprint as “a community of diverse human raters collectively serv[ing] as the ultimate evaluator and arbiter of what is helpful.” In its June 2025 announcement, the company stressed that “Humans [are] still in charge” because they are the ones rating the notes.

However, the number of active raters — the users who offer feedback on notes appended to tweets — has also decreased from its January 2025 peak. There are still almost half a million active raters (the task is, after all, easier), but the number of monthly ratings has fallen from more than 10 million to about 5 million.

X will need to increase the number of ratings to meet the growth in AI contributions and to gather enough data to provide a definitive helpfulness score to the notes being written.

Only a minority of notes are currently unpublished because they were actively deemed unhelpful; instead, almost 90% typically don’t receive enough ratings to be assessed one way or the other. Put differently, there’s an almost 9 in 10 chance that a note won’t be rated by enough other users to be even considered for publication.

X could turn to AI to start rating notes as well as writing them, removing the human bottleneck. But the company says this is not on the product roadmap.

"Our approach at X is to keep humans in control of which notes appear,” Coleman said, “while scaling note writing by giving people more ways to contribute.” This includes a currently embryonic effort at AI-drafted and human-edited notes.

Coleman added that, “AI delivers speed and scale; people provide the judgment on what’s helpful, informative, and worth showing."

Still, it is at least plausible that X could expand the role of AI in the pursuit of more published notes.

Rand of Cornell told Indicator that this is a natural, though not necessarily desirable, endpoint of Community Notes pursuing scale in fact-checking. First, it turned to the crowd because it determined there weren’t enough professional fact-checkers for the job at hand.5 Then, it adopted AI contributors because the crowd was ultimately insufficient. And now, as AI contributors appear ready to flood the zone with notes, it may need to use AI to rate notes in order to grow further.

In this scenario, X would have replaced disinformation labels applied through automated means by a handful of humans (its trust and safety team) with disinformation labels applied through automated means by a different handful of humans (the ones operating AI super contributors).

A daring effort to decentralize moderation would be de facto dead.

INDICATOR INFO BOX

  • I downloaded all Community Notes data on notes, users, and ratings from X’s public repository. AI contributors were identified by looking for the “APIEarnedIn” attribute. Most data in this analysis pertains to the period September 2025 to May 20 2026, unless otherwise specified.

  • I used Claude Code to extract all the sources linked and classify the language in all AI and human 60,988 helpful notes posted since the first synthetic one was published in September 2025. The script tagged each note's language with the open-source langdetect library and extracted cited sources by pulling every https:// URL from the note text. The Python script is available here.

  • To create a manageable corpus of tweets targeted by top contributors, I used another Python script I built with Claude Code to create a random sample, stratified by month, of more than 100 helpful notes from each of the top 10 AI and top 13 human contributors. I scraped each noted tweet to see whether it was live, who its author was, and how many views it received. This left me with a corpus of 1,500 tweets from 930 individual users for AI notes and 1,470 from 673 users for human notes.

  • I used Claude to identify elected officials, party accounts, or political candidates in my sample. I manually verified every single label by opening that account and searching for the owner on Google to find reliable sources confirming that they were political figures.
