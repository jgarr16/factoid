Today’s top stories

▸ The yield on American ten-year Treasurieshit its highest since 2007, at one point reaching 5.04%, in part because investors expect an interest-rate rise on Wednesday. The price of Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, rose above $108 a barrel, after Saudi Arabia curtailed shipments following a drone attack on its vital East-West oil pipeline. The price later fell slightly after positive data on oil inventories were released.

▸ The war in Iran has cost America$38bn so far, according to an estimate from the Congressional Budget Office. The nonpartisan scorekeeper reckons the conflict will cost America $2bn-3bn each month that it continues, and projected that high energy costs will increase inflation by 0.5% in the first quarter of 2027. It said the Pentagon did not respond to its requests for information.

▸ America’s Senate voted against advancing a bill to create a regulatory framework for cryptocurrency markets— a blow to the industry. The House of Representatives passed the Clarity Act last year, but the legislation has faced delays in the Senate, in part because Democrats are demanding that Mr Trump’s own crypto interests need to be controlled.

▸ Saudi Arabia said that a “red line” had been crossed, after its air force said it intercepted a Houthi drone near Mecca, Islam’s holiest place. The Yemeni militia denied it had targeted the city. Air-raid alerts were sounded across the kingdom on Tuesday; officials said that missile and drone attacks a day earlier had injured 13 people.

▸ OpenAI told Bloomberg that it is collaborating with two of its rivals, Anthropic and Google, to address fears over the safety of artificial intelligence. Calls for greater regulation of AI have intensified since Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s boss, published an essay on Saturday calling for a slowdown in the development of advanced models. Mr Trump has rejected the idea of a pause.

▸ Scott Bessent, America’s treasury secretary, said he would meet He Lifeng, his Chinese counterpart, in New York on Sunday, ahead of a summit between Mr Trump and Xi Jinping next week. Messrs Bessent and He are expected to discuss AI competition, rare earths and tariffs; a trade truce between America and China is set to expire in November but will probably be extended.

▸ All of the support acts on Ed Sheeran’s American tour quit after one of them, Macklemore, was dropped for expressing pro-Palestine views on stage. Mr Sheeran’s backing band also pulled out. Finneas, one of the opening acts, said that “artists must not be silenced when they speak up for the oppressed.” Mr Sheeran said the decision to drop Macklemore was made by the tour promoter.

The case for tightening in America

The Federal Reserve will probably raise interest rates on Wednesday. Markets put the chance above 90% after August’s consumer-price data, released last week, showed persistent price pressures. The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, is running even hotter. Core PCE, which strips out volatile food and energy prices, rose by 3.3% in the year to July. Meanwhile payroll growth exceeded expectations in August and the labour market remains strong, adding to the case for tighter policy.

Investors will also study the Fed’s projections for signs of further rate rises later this year. Policymakers may be reluctant to signal more if they see recent inflation as largely driven by high oil prices and tariffs. Kevin Warsh, the Fed chair, is unlikely to offer strong guidance. Even one rate rise would put him at odds with Donald Trump, who has repeatedly demanded lower rates. But with investors expecting action, holding steady risks damaging the Fed’s credibility— and provoking more bond-market bedlam.

Carlos Ghosn’s latest legal headache

An explosive corruption trial begins in Paris on Wednesday. Rachida Dati (pictured), a former culture minister, magistrate and mayor of the smart seventh arrondissement of Paris, faces accusations of “passive corruption and influence peddling”. Carlos Ghosn, a former boss of Renault-Nissan, in exile in Lebanon after fleeing charges of financial misconduct in Japan, is accused of “active corruption and influence peddling” as well as “abuse of power”. Both deny wrongdoing.

The case stems from a €300,000 ($346,248) per year contract awardedto Ms Dati by Mr Ghosn in 2009 for legal advice. Prosecutors allege that her employment concealed a “corrupt pact” under which she acted as a lobbyist at the European Parliament, of which she was a member at the time. If found guilty, Ms Dati, who recently ran for the mayorship of Paris, faces up to ten years in prison and a five-year electoral ban. Mr Ghosn is not expected to turn up for the trial.

Kosovo’s most famous politician faces the music

The trial of Hashim Thaci, Kosovo’s former president and first prime minister, ends on Wednesday. A special tribunal in The Hague will pronounce verdicts on crimes allegedly committed by Mr Thaci and the three other leaders of the guerrilla Kosovo Liberation Army, which fought for independence from Serbia in 1998-99. They include 102 murders: mostly of Serbs but also of ethnic Albanians, the majority group in Kosovo.

Mr Thaci argues that the KLA was a loosely run organisation and that there is no evidence of his wrongdoing. Among Kosovo’s Albanians the tribunal has sparked widespread anger.

Mr Thaci’s political party, currently in opposition, will be hoping to get a boost in the polls if its leader wins. Parliament recently failed to elect a new president. Mr Thaci’s supporters hold out for the possibility of his return. If legislators cannot agree, it will trigger a snap election. Mr Thaci, meanwhile, still faces another trial for witness tampering (something he denies), but that is expected to conclude very soon.

Europe’s regulatory roadmap

On Wednesday Ursula von der Leyen gives the annual “State of the Union” speech in Strasbourg, laying out the EU’s political direction for the year ahead. In her sixth such address, the president of the European Commission will probably talk up a proposal to ban children from social media, another push to loosen the EU’s growth-strangling mass of regulations, measures to redress trade imbalances with China, tighter migration controls and much more besides.

Watching as a guest of honour will be Mark Carney, the Canadian prime minister. As Canada’s defence spending rises, its reliance on Europe’s arms industry is increasing. Now Mr Carney is looking for ways to forge an even closer alliance as both sides adapt to a world in which America is no longer a reliable ally.

THE TRUSTEES OF THE NATURAL HISTORY MUSEUM, LONDON

The Natural History Museum looks to the future

The Natural History Museum in London excels at reviving the past. Visitors can walk beneath the 25m-long skeleton of a blue whale, gaze at meteorites that are older than Earth or watch a life-size animatronic T-Rex. Now the museum is bringing its own building back to life. On Wednesday it will reopen a gallery, closed since 1943, to display “Nature and Us”, a new exhibition. The NHM intends to open one new or renovated space each year until its 150th anniversary in 2031.

This approach has helped prevent the institution from going extinct. It had more than 7.1m visitors in 2025, a record for any British museum and a 13% rise on the previous year. Much of this success is attributed to its redesigned gardens, which opened in 2024. The NHM says its transformation signals an evolution “from being a catalogue of natural history to a catalyst for change”.
