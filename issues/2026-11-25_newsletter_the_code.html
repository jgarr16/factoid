Former xAI engineers let your agents travel back in time: Three founding members of Elon Musk's AI startup just launched Epochal, which wants AI to predict the future by learning the past. Its first product, Time Portal, is a point-in-time environment with three APIs. Search browses the web as of any date, and Fetch retrieves past versions of specific pages. Sandbox runs time-gated agents. That means you can backtest strategies, run forecasting evals, and build RL environments without future data leaking in. Preview it here.

Cognition closes the cyber gap on frontier labs: The team behind Devin just shipped SWE-2.1, bringing its biggest upgrades to cybersecurity. On ExploitBench, it scored 80, a major leap from its predecessor, though it still trails Claude Opus 5.5 at 91 and GPT-6.2 Sol's perfect 100. On CWE-Bench, however, it closes the gap to within two points of both frontier models. There's also an Ultrafast version that's 10x faster when you need a quick fix, plus SWE-2.1 Cyber for security teams. Pro, Max, and Teams users can try it at no cost until December 8. Check it out here.

T3 Code has been quietly tracking your coding data: If you run Claude Code or Codex through the agent orchestration platform called T3 Code, you should take note. A dev found that since March, the app has sent your model choices, token counts, and permission modes by default, with no consent prompt. Older versions also hashed account IDs from your Claude and Codex login files. Founder Theo says this only helped count users, and no prompts or files were ever sent. Version 0.1.12 stops reading those files, but telemetry stays on. If you handle sensitive code, here's what to do next.

An engineer’s guide to stand out in the AI era

Source: The Code, Superhuman

Standing out. Software engineer Leila Clark gets hundreds of resumes a month, often accompanied by near-identical AI-generated emails. With everyone using the same tools to look good on paper, Clark argues in a new essay that you're better off doing interesting engineering work and making sure people know about it.

Build better. Before adding another project to your portfolio, here's what Clark says makes one worth it:

  • Skip the filler. A fine-tuned YOLO model or a marginal NeurIPS paper won't necessarily impress someone who knows the field.

  • Think bigger. Pick something that takes weeks or months and teaches you things your peers haven't learned.

  • Follow your curiosity. If you think something is cool, it probably is.

  • Ask for harder projects. Your manager might not assign you ambitious work unless you express interest.

Spread the word. You've spent weeks building something interesting. Now make sure it doesn't just sit on GitHub.

  • Find your crowd. A niche meetup with 20 people can be more valuable than a room packed with 100.

  • Explain your thinking. A blog post can explain why you made the choices behind what you built.

  • Share it widely. Post on Hacker News, Reddit, or Lobsters, and send it to friends and mentors.

It works. Clark's former colleague Nolen worked on fairly unglamorous internal infrastructure, yet almost everyone at the company knew him. He did great work and wrote about it. When he left to build silly web games, he kept blogging. His best game eventually made it into the New York Times!

The payoff. The well-known engineers Clark knows don't even apply for jobs, because people approach them with roles already in mind. Getting there leans heavily on writing, and veteran engineer Gergely Orosz's guide to becoming a better writer is a good place to start.
